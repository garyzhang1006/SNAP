\subsection{Common step rule}
\label{app:schedule}
For each of the 125 configurations, we selected the largest released checkpoint step available for all three runs. The analysis records that step in each reduced filename, although the supplement described in the analysis record omits the fetch stage's complete configuration table. In 92 configurations, the three runs have different maximum steps. DataDecide stops the two auxiliary runs below 1B at 25 percent of the 1B compute budget and trains three complete runs only at 1B \citep{magnusson2025}. The per-instance release contains auxiliary runs from 150M upward, so the five replicated sizes used here begin at 150M rather than 4M. This availability rule explains the difference from DataDecide's fourteen-size ladder.

In 26 configurations, one at 530M and 25 at 750M, the largest maximum step exceeds the smallest by more than a factor of 1.5. We indexed the member names of the 25 released recipe archives to recover every run's maximum step, and the index reproduces the selected step recorded for all 375 runs. At 530M the selected step averages 86.7 percent of the default run's final step, with configuration values from 38.9 to 88.7 percent. At 750M it averages 41.5 percent, with values from 17.7 to 44.0 percent. Only 33 configurations have three runs that share a final step, and 24 of those sit at 1B.

Table~\ref{tab:truncation} recomputes both estimates on subsets defined by the index. Excluding the 26 severely truncated configurations raises margin inflation to 1.276 and accuracy inflation to 1.120, and neither wild interval includes one. The 33 configurations with a shared final step give intervals too wide to separate inflation from one, and 18 of their 24 recipes contribute only a 1B configuration. We also define a configuration's influence as $(T_c-\widehat\Lam^2U_c)/\sum_{c'}U_{c'}$, which sums to zero and measures how far the configuration pulls the squared ratio. Its Spearman correlation with the selected step's share of the default final step is $-0.036$ for margins and $0.001$ for accuracy. Permuting that share within each size over 4,999 draws gives $p=0.314$ and $p=0.990$. Within the two truncated bands, which hold 25 configurations each, the correlations are $-0.011$ and $0.155$ for margins and $0.275$ and $0.232$ for accuracy, and we didn't test those band values separately. We chose these subsets after seeing the full-sample estimates, so they describe sensitivity and don't test a stated hypothesis. The auxiliary-run contrast and the estimates by size in Table~\ref{tab:original7} give two further checks.

\subsection{Decision checks and deviations}
The planning document lists nine checks with proposed responses. Its claimed date is September 5, 2026, but we haven't established that date through an independent timestamp. We consequently describe the document as a reported plan and treat its checks as exploratory assessments.

A batch offset can contribute to the same cross-trait products as run-level variation. The auxiliary-run contrast removes the shared default-versus-auxiliary contrast from that estimate, while the estimate using all three runs remains exposed. Check G8 passes on point estimates, although the intervals cross the decision boundaries. We haven't corrected the full-set estimate for this exposure.

Under the default gain simulator, $\sigma_g=0.05$ produces approximately 61 percent of the observed margin excess. An empirically matched simulator produces approximately eight percent at the same gain scale and six percent at the estimated scale of 0.042. These results describe sensitivity to simulator assumptions and aren't causal bounds on the contribution of gain. The accuracy result offers no independent protection against this mechanism because multiplying a margin by a positive factor leaves its sign unchanged. Partialling the gain covariate increases $\Lambda_M$, and adding gain to the rank-one predictor worsens held-out point error relative to the scalar predictor alone. These observations don't identify a unique causal explanation.

\subsection{Excluded populations}
MultiBERTs provides 25 seeds for one configuration \citep{sellam2022}, whereas the 100 BERT runs of \citet{mccoy2020} vary fine-tuning from one pretrained checkpoint. Pythia's size ladder lacks seed replicates \citep{biderman2023}, and the OLMo-2 checkpoints considered here lack replicates at matched steps \citep{teamolmo2025}. The signal-and-noise panel lacks per-item outputs, which limits its use to the external check. We exclude these populations because they lack the matched replicates or item outputs required here.

\subsection{Relation to prior measurement work}
\citet{reimers2017} compare score distributions across seeds within datasets, while \citet{madaan2024} report benchmark-level seed variance. \citet{hagmann2023} model nondeterminism with scalar variance components and their interactions with data properties. Our target adds covariance between benchmark-specific run deviations to that measurement problem.

\citet{jordan2024} attributes much test-set variance to per-example variation across seeds, and its Appendix C compares run accuracy across five ImageNet evaluation sets with $R^2$ at most 0.14. Those comparisons concern shifted versions of one benchmark. \citet{bui2025} vary fine-tuning seeds, and \citet{hochlehnert2025} vary decoding seeds, which address different variance components from the pretraining-run variation examined here. \citet[Section 4.3]{dodge2020} ask whether a seed performs well across datasets and compare four GLUE task rankings without estimating a covariance matrix.

\citet[Figure 3]{zhou2020} report a Spearman correlation matrix across datasets. Their standard block has correlations at least 0.95, while standard-to-analysis entries include 0.97 for STR-L and 0.30 for STR-O. These correlations compare checkpoints within runs and average across seeds, so they combine changes in checkpoint performance rather than isolating within-configuration seed deviations. \citet{dufour2026} separate training and sampling variation in FID, while \citet{miller2024} estimates item-level uncertainty for one model. \citet{zhao2026} report the three-seed standard deviation of a twelve-task average and combine twelve per-task Welch tests in their Appendix E.4 without estimating cross-task covariance.

\citet{haupt2026} use the PolyPythias 410M panel to estimate per-item variation and prioritise items by welfare weight and improvability. Their per-item variance estimates and approximate item-independence assumption address a different quantity from the off-diagonal covariance of benchmark run effects. Their item-level estimates and our benchmark covariance therefore answer different questions using some of the same models.

\citet{cheverud2001}, \citet{nyholt2004}, and \citet{li2005} estimate effective numbers of independent tests from observed correlation matrices. Cheverud uses eigenvalue variance, Nyholt uses spectral decomposition of linkage disequilibrium, and Li and Ji revise the effective-count approximation. Their application adjusts significance thresholds, whereas ours converts an estimated noise covariance into an aggregate standard deviation and an effective benchmark count. \citet{searle1992} explain negative variance-component estimates from moment methods when the true component lies near zero.

\citet{schaeffer2023} show how discontinuous metrics can change the appearance of performance trends. Accuracy thresholds our margin at zero, so we report both scales while retaining their separate noise properties. Low estimated seed signal relative to item noise provides a measurement limitation for accuracy in this study. \citet{dehghani2021} show that benchmark selection can reorder methods, which concerns the composition sensitivity examined here.

\citet{demsar2006} and \citet{dror2017} develop inference across datasets, while \citet{card2020} examine statistical power and \citet{gorman2019} examine dependence on a single split. \citet{burnell2023} advocate per-instance reporting alongside aggregate scores. \citet{henderson2018} and \citet{agarwal2021} address seed sensitivity and uncertainty in reinforcement learning. The authors resample runs within tasks, with an additional task-resampling variant in their Appendix A.5, while our application requires preservation of paired run effects across benchmarks.

\citet{hardy2026} apply generalizability theory to 4,000 released models, which places replication across models rather than training seeds. \citet{kohli2026} estimates roughly two independent votes among nine frontier judges, and \citet{messing2026} reports naive standard errors that are 40 to 60 percent too small after accounting for judge and prompt variation. \citet{sha2026} estimate how many independent signals a leaderboard's benchmark scores provide across models. Those studies measure dependence across models, judges, or prompts, while we measure paired benchmark deviations across training runs.

The cross-half identity builds on the repeated-measurement approach to attenuation described by \citet{spearman1904}. Under independent errors between halves, paired measurements remove the item-noise contribution from covariance estimates, including diagonal entries. \citet{cronbach1951} relates split-half reliability to random halves of an item population, which motivates the reliability table and the 50 re-splits in N3. Those re-splits change the margin estimate by a reported standard deviation of 0.002.

The \citet{brown1910} and \citet{spearman1910} test-length formula contains $1+(k-1)\rho$, the same algebraic form used for $\Lambda^2$. Its correlation concerns parallel items, whereas ours concerns benchmark run effects. Substituting the reliability means in Table~\ref{tab:original3} at $k=10$ would produce 6.87 and 3.91, which aren't estimates of our variance inflation.

\citet{campbell1959} compare traits and measurement methods in a crossed matrix. The within-format and cross-format summaries here resemble heterotrait blocks, but no benchmark appears in multiple formats. This design therefore can't separate benchmark content from scoring format or establish format as the cause of the association. \citet{brennan2001} provides the variance-component framework for these distinctions.

\citet{hofmann2025} fit benchmark-specific item-response models to an overlapping evaluation panel, with seven authors shared with \citet{heineman2025}. \citet{kipnis2024} estimate a factor across six benchmarks that correlates 0.94 with total score. Those ability structures describe performance differences rather than the within-configuration noise covariance estimated here. \citet{hedges2010} estimate uncertainty for dependent effect sizes without requiring their complete covariance matrix, while our application estimates the covariance needed for an effective-count point estimate.

\paragraph{Permutation comparison}
N2 was proposed as an estimator-bias check rather than the primary inferential procedure. In the reported 500 permutations, none reaches the observed margin estimate, and 0.028 reach the accuracy estimate. This reassignment holds the observed configurations fixed while independently permuting trait-specific run labels. The primary bootstrap instead targets variation across recipe clusters, so the two procedures use different randomisation and sampling assumptions. We report both accuracy results because the disagreement depends on their different randomisation and sampling assumptions.

\paragraph{Associations with scoring format}
The cross-format diagnostic retains the diagonal sum and only those off-diagonal terms whose traits have different formats. It has a null reference of one when those retained covariances vanish, but the masked sum needn't represent the variance of a realisable benchmark average. Because each benchmark has only one format, the comparison can't identify a causal effect of format.

The separate calculation reproduces the full estimate to four decimal places before retaining 54 of the 90 ordered off-diagonal pairs. It gives 0.921 with interval 0.801 to 1.035 for margins, and 0.965 with interval 0.848 to 1.063 for accuracy. Both fall below the proposed 1.2 threshold, and the margin result falls below the 1.153 expected from uniformly distributed covariance contributions under the comparison used here.

BoolQ is the only yes/no benchmark, so all its off-diagonal pairs cross a format boundary. Its margin seed variance is $1.43\times10^{-3}$, compared with $4.34\times10^{-4}$ for the next-largest trait. Excluding BoolQ gives a cross-format margin diagnostic of 1.006 with interval 0.972 to 1.039, compared with an unrestricted nine-trait estimate of 1.786 with interval 1.689 to 1.877.

Among the nine traits with defined margin correlations, excluding WinoGrande, the mean correlation is 0.624 across sixteen within-format pairs and 0.007 across twenty cross-format pairs. The three cloze traits give margin inflation of 1.225 with interval 1.064 to 1.367. Excluding ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge from the multiple-choice block leaves inflation of 1.479 with interval 1.416 to 1.541. These associations show concentration within the larger format groups, while benchmark content and format remain confounded. A rank-one model with trait-specific loadings can also produce uneven contributions across those groups.

\paragraph{Scoring-scale interventions}
\label{app:scale}
We transformed the saved margins item by item and recomputed each estimate with the items, runs, split, and interval routines held fixed. For benchmark $j$, $s_j$ is the standard deviation of its per-item margins pooled across all configurations, runs, and items, and it ranges from 0.087 for WinoGrande to 0.468 for CommonsenseQA. Table~\ref{tab:scale} reports the untransformed margins, division by $s_j$, clipping at $\pm s_j$ before that division, two hyperbolic tangent scores, and the sign of each margin. Multiplying every margin by ten leaves the estimate unchanged to nine decimal places, which checks the construction.

\paragraph{Performance correlation as a noise proxy}
The reported Frobenius distances between $R_E$ and $R_P$ are 5.59 for margins and 6.49 for accuracy. Negative estimated variances leave eighteen margin and 34 accuracy ordered off-diagonal correlations undefined. The implementation fills these entries with zero before differencing, including undefined diagonal entries compared with unit diagonals in $R_P$. Those diagonal substitutions contribute 1.0 and 2.0 to the squared distances.

Repairing only those diagonals gives distances of 5.499 and 6.339. Restricting the comparison to jointly defined entries gives 4.374 and 4.593, which still exceed the planned threshold of 0.5. The threshold alone offers limited discrimination between different ten-trait matrices, so the entrywise comparisons provide a more useful description.

The off-diagonal association between the matrices is 0.756 for margins and 0.345 for accuracy. Mean defined $R_E$ entries are 0.281 and 0.273, compared with all-pair $R_P$ means of 0.750 and 0.754. On matching pairs, the $R_P$ means are 0.743 and 0.769. The all-pair comparison therefore differs in scale by factors of approximately 2.7 and 2.8. The unadjusted proxy predicts a mean margin aggregate standard deviation of 0.0072, compared with the reported 0.0057.

An additional exploratory baseline, P2 R, rescales $R_P$ off-diagonals by the training-fold ratio of mean noise correlation to mean performance correlation. Its mean factors are 0.366 for margins and 0.370 for accuracy, compared with whole-population ratios of 0.375 and 0.362. On the same five recipe folds, its squared prediction errors are 0.0131 and 0.0342. It improves on P2 but remains 2.3 times worse than P1 on margins and worse than independence on accuracy. Its direction relative to independence holds on margins in all 1,000 random fold partitions and on accuracy in 987 of them, but the recipe-bootstrap intervals below include zero on both scales. This rescaling also requires replicated runs to estimate its factor.

\paragraph{Paired uncertainty for the prediction comparison}\label{app:paired}

\paragraph{Interval construction and cluster sensitivity}
A percentile bootstrap over 125 configurations gives intervals of 1.069 to 1.426 for margins and 0.976 to 1.182 for accuracy. The margin interval excludes one, and the accuracy interval includes one. Comparing squared linearised standard errors, using 0.045 against 0.094 on margins and 0.038 against 0.052 on accuracy, gives ratios of 0.23 and 0.53. The implementation separately reports numerator-based design effects \citep{kish1965} of 2.06 and 1.26 from $1+4\rho_{\rm ICC}$, compared with the planned 1.60.

These ratios describe different quantities and aren't contradictory estimates of one design effect. The numerator calculation uses $T_c$, while linearisation uses $T_c-\widehat\Lambda^2U_c$. The interval procedures also change resampling unit, studentisation, and denominator treatment together. We consequently don't infer a single intraclass correlation from their relative widths. The bootstrap-t addresses concerns associated with few-cluster approximations, as discussed by \citet{bell2002}, but it doesn't guarantee coverage at 25 clusters.

\paragraph{Observed gaps and predicted rank reversals}
Using accuracy noise $\sigma_{\rm agg}=0.00514$, the Gaussian calculation gives $P_{\rm flip}>0.05$ for a true gap below 0.01196. Among the $5\binom{25}{2}=1{,}500$ observed same-size pairs, 455 have observed gaps below this threshold, or 30.3 percent. The proportions range from 28.7 to 33.3 percent across bands. Substituting observed gaps meets the stated numerical criterion, but noisy observed gaps can move pairs across the threshold in either direction. This count therefore isn't a lower bound on the fraction of true gaps below the threshold, and it doesn't directly measure realised reversal frequency. A practitioner faces the operational form of the same question, which is whether correcting their own test for this covariance helps once the correction carries the noise of a three-run design. We simulated that question directly rather than arguing it. Each of 4,000 replicates builds the shipped design, estimates the ratio from it, and then applies three rules to a fresh comparison of two model averages, which are an uncorrected rule that assumes benchmark independence, a plug-in rule that multiplies its standard error by the estimate, and an oracle rule that uses the true value. On a margin-like population the uncorrected rule declares a difference in 0.113 of replicates when the true gap is zero, against its nominal 0.05, while the plug-in rule declares in 0.051 and the oracle in 0.050. Three runs per model leave those at 0.115, 0.053 and 0.051. The estimate carries a standard deviation of 0.059 across replicates and still lands within 0.001 of the oracle, so its own noise costs almost nothing at this design. On an accuracy-like population the gap is smaller and the estimate noisier, at a standard deviation of 0.099, and there the three rules give 0.067, 0.052 and 0.047. The plug-in rule overshoots the oracle by 0.005 there, which is the price of estimating a correction that is itself close to one. A practitioner who accepts that correction will ask next how many runs their own comparison needs, and whether buying runs also repairs the miscalibration, so we swept the same three rules over one to ten runs per model at true gaps of zero, a quarter, a half and one aggregate standard deviation, with 4,000 replicates per combination. Extra runs buy nothing against the size error, because the true standard error and the independence standard error both fall with the square root of the run count and their ratio doesn't move at all. The sweep says so directly, since the uncorrected rule declares at a zero margin gap in between 0.112 and 0.122 of replicates across all ten run counts, while the corrected rule stays between 0.047 and 0.056, and the accuracy population gives 0.061 to 0.078 against 0.047 to 0.063. Neither sequence trends with the run budget, and each individual rate carries a Monte Carlo standard error near 0.004, so what looks like movement is the simulation rather than the design. Power is the thing that additional runs actually buy here. At a gap of one aggregate standard deviation the corrected rule declares in 0.100 of replicates with one run per model and in 0.614 with ten, while the accuracy population gives 0.111 and 0.615 at the same two budgets. No run count up to ten reaches four fifths at any of the three nonzero gaps, so a calibrated comparison at this battery costs more runs than the release supplies, and the correction fixes the error rate rather than the sample size. A second sweep carried the same design out to 80 runs per model and found the size error exactly where it had been, since across 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 60 and 80 runs the uncorrected rule declares at a zero margin gap in between 0.108 and 0.119 of replicates while the corrected rule holds between 0.047 and 0.053. Four fifths arrives at 16 runs per model for a margin gap of one aggregate standard deviation, which is exactly what the ordinary normal calculation for that gap gives, and at 20 runs on the accuracy population, where the oracle rule also misses four fifths at 16 runs by 0.002. The extra accuracy runs therefore pay for the normal approximation rather than for the estimated correction. A gap of half a standard deviation needs 63 runs per model by the same calculation, and the nearest count above it that we simulated, 80, reaches 0.881 on margins. A third sweep took the quarter standard deviation gap out to 360 runs per model and found four fifths at exactly the 252 runs the closed form predicts for margins, and at 300 on the accuracy population, where even the oracle rule falls a fraction short at 252. The uncorrected rule still declares at a zero margin gap in between 0.112 and 0.122 of replicates at every one of those seven counts, so the size error survives every run budget from one run per model to 360. Those are the numbers a replicate study should be sized against, and all of them sit far above the three runs per configuration that this release ships. Both sweeps estimate the correction on the same population that the comparison is drawn from, which is the friendly case and not the one a reader is in. Anyone applying this correction will estimate the ratio once, on one battery and one model family, and will then carry it to a comparison whose own dependence they haven't measured, so we measured that transfer directly. The correction was estimated from the shipped margin design, whose true ratio is 1.245, and it was applied to comparisons whose own true ratio was swept from 1.00 to 1.60, with 8,000 replicates per target and a matched rule re-estimated on the target for comparison. The transferred rule holds nominal size only where the two ratios agree, at 0.053 when the target is 1.245 itself. Below that it turns conservative rather than wrong, declaring in 0.015 of replicates at a target of 1.00 and 0.028 at 1.10, and it pays for that in power, since a gap of one aggregate standard deviation at a target of 1.00 is declared in 0.043 of replicates against 0.114 for the matched rule. Above it the rule under-corrects and the size error comes back, at 0.080 for a target of 1.40, 0.101 at 1.50 and 0.123 at 1.60, against 0.159, 0.188 and 0.216 for no correction at all. The matched rule holds between 0.048 and 0.053 across the entire sweep. The correction is therefore not a constant that a reader can borrow from this paper. What transfers is the procedure rather than the number, and a practitioner whose battery is more dependent than ours would keep most of the error they were trying to remove. Repeating the whole sweep with the accuracy design as the source, whose true ratio is 1.078 and whose estimate carries a standard deviation of 0.098, gives the same shape from the other end. That correction holds nominal size at 0.056 for a target of 1.10, which is its own neighbourhood, and it recovers almost nothing above that, at 0.168 for a target of 1.50 against 0.199 with no correction at all and 0.054 for a matched rule. The penalty is therefore governed by the distance between the two ratios rather than by which design produced the number. Reading both sweeps together, the transferred rule keeps its declare rate between 0.04 and 0.06 only while the target ratio sits within about 0.05 of the source, which is a narrow enough window that we recommend measuring rather than borrowing.

\paragraph{Simulation and permutation calibration}
The linearised margin standard error of 0.045 is close to the N1 null dispersion of 0.046. Accuracy gives 0.038 against the simulated 0.048, a difference of approximately 21 percent. The reported interval half-widths are 0.0974 and 0.082, approximately 2.2 standard errors compared with the $t(24)$ critical value of 2.06. Substituting the accuracy simulation dispersion gives a descriptive interval of 0.979 to 1.178, which also includes one. This substitution isn't a calibrated alternative interval because the simulated and observed populations differ.

A separate implementation of the interval definitions uses 25 clusters of five configurations, three runs, and ten traits. It assumes exchangeable seed covariance, independent item noise across halves, and recipe heterogeneity in seed variance. Across sixteen cells formed from four true correlations between zero and 0.10 and four noise settings, 200 datasets per cell give empirical coverage from 0.920 to 0.975. Each cell lies within two Monte Carlo standard errors of 0.95, and estimated inflation is within 0.01 of truth in fifteen cells. Four cells with dispersion 0.044, compared with observed null dispersions 0.046 and 0.048, have coverage 0.950, 0.935, 0.975, and 0.960. Their interval widths range from 0.203 to 0.238, compared with the reported margin width of 0.195. This limited simulation checks the procedure under its specified populations and doesn't validate all departures present in the study.

A second simulation, run for this revision with 2,000 replicates per population and 4,999 draws, scores four interval procedures on the same simulated moments. Table~\ref{tab:coverage} lists the populations, each with 125 configurations in 25 recipe clusters, three runs, and ten benchmarks. The wild bootstrap-$t$ covers between 0.928 and 0.954. The cluster-robust $t(24)$ tracks it within 0.02 and falls to 0.911 with one recipe at six times the variance. The configuration percentile bootstrap falls to 0.872 under recipe-shared run effects, and the recipe percentile bootstrap covers between 0.857 and 0.940 with undefined draws in the accuracy-like noise cells. Cross-half error correlation of 0.1 biases the estimate by $-0.014$ and $-0.016$, and its margin-like wild interval misses above in 0.066 of replicates. The Monte Carlo standard error of a coverage near 0.95 is 0.005.

We later added a cluster test-inversion interval, a family the analysis plan lists for ratios with an uncertain denominator. For each candidate $\theta=\Lambda^2$, it forms the recipe sums $z_g=\sum_{c\in g}(T_c-\theta U_c)$ and keeps $\theta$ when the cluster-robust $t$ statistic of their mean, with a $G/(G-1)$ factor, stays within the $t(24)$ critical value. The accepted set can be unbounded, and we record its shape instead of truncating it. Rerunning the R6 generator with the same seeds reproduces the cluster-$t$ coverage of every row in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}, so the inversion column scores the same 24,000 simulated datasets. The inversion covers between 0.932 and 0.957, with median widths within 0.006 of the wild interval, and it holds 0.955 with one recipe at six times the variance, where the wild interval gives 0.930. Its set is bounded in 23,999 of those datasets, and the exception is one Student-$t$ replicate. A restricted variant that imposes $\theta$ in the variance without centring covers between 0.946 and 0.970, which is above 0.95 in eleven of the twelve populations and more than two Monte Carlo standard errors above it in eight.

The Monte Carlo standard error of 0.005 at 2,000 replicates is the size of the shortfalls that Table~\ref{tab:coverage} shows, so we repeated four cells with 10,000 replicates and new seeds. They are the two cells calibrated to the observed estimates and the two with the lowest wild coverage. The wild interval covers 0.933 with cross-half correlation 0.1, 0.935 with one dominant recipe, 0.940 in the margin-like cell, and 0.945 in the accuracy-like cell, each with a Monte Carlo standard error near 0.0025. We later repeated these four cells a second time at 10,000 replicates with a third seed, which returns 0.937, 0.935, 0.951 and 0.947. Three of them agree with the first run inside one standard error. The margin-like cell doesn't, because 0.940 and 0.951 sit more than three standard errors of their difference apart, so we ran that one cell a third time at 40,000 replicates, which returns 0.947 with a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.0011 and puts the residual-scaled version at 0.955. Pooling all three runs of that cell over 60,000 replicates gives 0.946, so it is the mildest of the four rather than a settled 0.940. The cross-half and dominant-recipe cells repeat within 0.004, and they carry the shortfall that matters here. In the cross-half cell the wild interval misses above in 0.054 of replicates against a nominal 0.025, so the shortfall falls on one side. The centred inversion covers 0.935, 0.956, 0.942, and 0.947 in the same four cells, so the inversion keeps its advantage of 0.021 under one dominant recipe while matching the wild interval elsewhere. We then repeated the other eight cells at the same precision, which puts every cell of Table~\ref{tab:coverage} on 10,000 replicates for these four procedures. No cell moves by more than 0.011 against its 2,000-replicate value. Across the twelve, the wild interval covers 0.933 to 0.953, the cluster-robust $t$ covers 0.916 to 0.949, the centred inversion covers 0.935 to 0.956, and the restricted inversion covers 0.951 to 0.970. The restricted inversion covers at least as much as the centred one, which covers at least as much as the wild interval, which covers at least as much as the cluster-robust $t$, in eleven of the twelve cells. The exception is the cell with mean correlation 0.2, where the wild interval beats the centred inversion by 0.002. The three lowest wild coverages are 0.933 and 0.936 in the two cross-half correlation cells and 0.935 with one dominant recipe. A later sweep repeated all twelve populations once more at 10,000 replicates with a third seed, alongside the residual-scaled construction that the next paragraph compares, and it puts the wild interval between 0.929 and 0.950. That second sweep agrees with this one to within 0.004 at each end, so the shortfall we describe here is a property of the construction rather than of one set of draws.

Coverage in simulation says which construction a reader should trust, and it doesn't say how far the reported interval would move under a different one, so we applied all eight to the data itself. Each wild construction ran 9,999 draws over twenty bootstrap seeds, and no endpoint varies across those seeds by a standard deviation above 0.0023. That matters most for the accuracy conclusion, whose lower endpoint of 0.9933 is the one number that decides whether the full-battery interval includes one. Its standard deviation across the twenty seeds is 0.0015, so the endpoint sits more than four of its own bootstrap standard deviations below one, and the inclusion of one is a property of the data rather than of the draws. On the full battery the margin interval runs from 1.141 to 1.339 under Rademacher weights, 1.166 to 1.344 under Mammen weights, 1.142 to 1.338 under Webb weights, and 1.130 to 1.349 under leave-one-cluster-out residual scaling, with the cluster-robust $t$ at 1.147 to 1.334, the centred inversion at 1.095 to 1.323, and the restricted inversion at 1.074 to 1.329. Every one of the eight excludes one on that scale. On accuracy every one of the eight includes one instead, and the widest of them, the restricted inversion, runs from 0.988 to 1.169. Removing BoolQ leaves both scales above one under all eight, where the widest margin interval is again the restricted inversion at 1.391 to 1.840. The shipped interval, which draws 4,999 times rather than 9,999, sits within 0.003 of the Rademacher mean in all four batteries. The draw count doesn't reach coverage either, since scoring 999, 2,499, 4,999 and 19,999 draws on identical replicates leaves coverage at 0.945 to 0.946 on an independent population and 0.941 to 0.943 at the band-shared bound, with median widths inside 0.002 of each other. A replicator on a laptop can therefore use the cheapest of those counts without changing a conclusion, and that run used seed 20260980 at 4,000 replicates. Every coverage number we report is a 95 percent number, so a further run asks whether the procedure is calibrated or merely tuned at that one level. Scoring the same replicates at one in ten, one in twenty and one in a hundred gives 0.896, 0.951 and 0.990 on an independent population, and 0.896, 0.947 and 0.986 at the band-shared bound, so the interval tracks the level it claims across the tails. The violation breaks all three levels together rather than one of them, since a band-shared quarter gives 0.743, 0.828 and 0.928, which is the same shortfall the main text reports at 95 percent. That run used seed 20260979 at 4,000 replicates. Which of these intervals include one, and which exclude it, doesn't depend on the construction we report.

None of that repairs the wild interval's own shortfall, so we asked whether a different construction would. We compared six of them on the same twelve populations with 4,000 replicates each, which are the wild bootstrap-t under Rademacher, Mammen and Webb weights, a wild bootstrap-t whose recipe residual is divided by $1-h_g$ with $h_g$ the recipe's share of the denominator sum, the cluster-robust $t$ with the $G/(G-1)$ correction, and the centred inversion. We ran four cells first, which are the two with the lowest wild coverage and the two calibrated to the observed estimates, and there the residual-scaled version reached nominal coverage in three of them while no other construction reached it in more than one, so we then ran the remaining eight to see whether the advantage survived, and it did. Across all twelve at that replicate count it covers between 0.943 and 0.958, against 0.932 to 0.951 for the shipped Rademacher version. We then repeated both of those two constructions on all twelve populations at 10,000 replicates, which cuts the Monte Carlo standard error to 0.002 and puts the comparison at one precision on both sides. The residual-scaled version covers between 0.940 and 0.959 there, against 0.929 to 0.950 for the shipped one, and it improves every cell by between 0.007 and 0.014. It reaches 0.95 in ten cells where the shipped version reaches it in one, and its intervals run 4 to 13 percent wider at the median. Mammen weights cover between 0.916 and 0.942 and lose to Rademacher in every cell, Webb weights land within 0.003 of Rademacher everywhere, and the corrected cluster-robust $t$ covers between 0.923 and 0.950. The residual-scaled version also matches or beats the centred inversion in all twelve, by up to 0.012. We picked that construction after seeing the first four cells, so the eight that followed confirm it on the same generator family rather than test it independently, and we therefore report the shipped interval throughout and record this one as the better-calibrated alternative. On the observed data it gives \ci{1.130}{1.349} for margins and \ci{0.987}{1.163} for accuracy, so it moves no conclusion, except that its upper endpoint erases the 0.011 of separation from the planned threshold that we report below.

A reader can also ask what the reported interval becomes once its own miscalibration is corrected on these data, rather than through a construction chosen in simulation. We applied Beran prepivoting, which runs a second wild bootstrap of 1,999 draws inside each of 1,999 outer resamples, and reads the outer quantiles at the levels the inner distribution implies. On the full battery the margin interval calibrates to \ci{1.151}{1.328}, which runs 0.018 narrower than the shipped \ci{1.143}{1.338} and still excludes one. Accuracy calibrates to \ci{0.994}{1.161} and still includes one, the BoolQ-removed margin interval calibrates to \ci{1.689}{1.878}, and the BoolQ-removed accuracy interval widens to \ci{1.396}{1.704} while staying above one. The implied coverage of the nominal levels runs from 0.925 to 0.977 across the four batteries, so this correction moves no conclusion, and on the full battery it disagrees with the simulation about the direction of the error. Prepivoting measures calibration inside the resampling model that the interval already assumes, so a violation the bootstrap shares with the estimator stays invisible to it, and the simulated coverage remains the figure a reader should weigh. We tested that explanation rather than leaving it as an argument. Running the same prepivoting inside the four simulated cells that carry the shortfall, with 1,000 replicates and 399 draws at each level, gives coverage of 0.924, 0.933, 0.931 and 0.944, against 0.938, 0.931, 0.936 and 0.953 for the shipped interval on the same replicates. An uncalibrated interval at the same 399 draws covers 0.936, 0.928, 0.938 and 0.949, so the draw count isn't doing the work. The calibration tracks the interval it corrects and repairs none of the shortfall, which is what a correction blind to the violation would do.

The estimator assumes that item noise in one half doesn't correlate with item noise in the other, so we asked how much a violation could manufacture. Holding the true seed correlation at zero, we swept the within-benchmark cross-half noise correlation from $-0.1$ to 0.5 with 1,000 replicates at each value. The mean margin estimate stays between 0.997 and 1.001 across that whole range, and no margin replicate reaches the observed 1.244. What the correlation changes is dispersion, which falls from 0.057 at zero to 0.046 at 0.5 on margins. Noise correlated this way adds the same quantity to the numerator and the denominator, so it can't create inflation and instead pulls a real inflation toward one, which is the $-0.014$ bias that Table~\ref{tab:coverage} records. On accuracy the null dispersion is wide enough that 0.19 of replicates reach the observed 1.078 with no correlation at all, and that share falls to 0.07 at a correlation of 0.5. This sweep says nothing about noise shared across benchmarks within a half and repeated in the opposite half, which adds the same quantity to every entry of the cross-half covariance, so the numerator absorbs all of it while the denominator absorbs only the diagonal tenth.

We measured that second violation in the same way as the first. Each half received a scalar effect that every benchmark loads on, repeated exactly in the opposite half, and we swept its share of item-noise variance with 2,000 replicates at each value and no seed covariance. The mean margin estimate climbs monotonically from 0.999 at a share of zero to 1.606 at a share of 0.4, and a share near 0.124 reproduces the observed 1.244. At a share of 0.05 only 0.007 of replicates reach the observed value, so small amounts of this contamination don't explain the margin result. Accuracy is far more fragile, because the same construction reaches the observed 1.078 at a share near 0.009, which is about 1 percent of item-noise variance. We haven't measured this component in the released scores, so these figures give the contamination a reader would have to assume rather than a bound we established. The passage-aware split addresses shared structure inside one benchmark and leaves this cross-benchmark channel open. We tried to detect the contamination in the data and failed. Regressing the off-diagonal entries of the estimated seed covariance on the item-noise outer product gives a slope of 1.675 on margins, with a recipe bootstrap interval of 0.534 to 3.068, and 0.033 on accuracy with an interval of $-0.006$ to 0.073. Simulations with the observed covariance and no shared effect give a mean slope of 1.682 on margins, and adding the shared effect at the share that reproduces the observed estimate moves that mean only to 1.808, against a replicate spread of 1.290 to 2.371. The statistic can't separate the two explanations, because a seed covariance that follows the item-noise scales looks the same as contamination that follows them. We therefore report that diagnostic as having no power rather than as a clean check.

One part of the assumption does admit a direct test, and we ran it. Write the per-item score of run $r$ in configuration $c$ as $x_{cri}$, and let $\mu_{ci}$ be its mean over that configuration's three runs, so that $d_{cri}=x_{cri}-\mu_{ci}$ carries the seed effect together with the item noise. Permuting $d$ across the items of one group, separately for every run, leaves each group mean and therefore each trait score exactly where it was, and it leaves any component that all items of a group share exactly where it was as well, because permuting a constant returns the constant. What the permutation destroys is the tie between particular items and particular runs, which is the channel a shared passage or a shared source document travels through. Over 2,000 permutations of the 37,682 items in their 66 groups, the margin estimate has mean 1.2428 with a standard deviation of 0.0009, and 0.087 of the permutations reach or exceed the observed 1.244, with a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.006 on that share. On accuracy the permutation mean is 1.0763 with a standard deviation of 0.0053, and 0.352 of them reach or exceed the observed value. The item-level channel therefore accounts for about 0.001 of the margin estimate and 0.002 of the accuracy estimate, against excesses over one of 0.244 and 0.078. Re-drawing the item half split 200 times moves the estimate further on the real data than under permutation, where the real split standard deviation of 0.0018 on margins beats the 0.0009 the permuted data give, and 0.0083 on accuracy beats 0.0055, so item-level structure does survive and it widens the spread without moving the centre. The shipped split returns 1.244 on margins against a re-split median of 1.246 and a range of 1.242 to 1.254, and 1.078 on accuracy against a median of 1.083 and a range of 1.059 to 1.103, so the reported numbers aren't a favourable draw. An effect that every item of a benchmark carries survives this permutation untouched, so the whole-benchmark form of the violation stays untested and only the sub-benchmark form is bounded here.

\paragraph{Availability of a separate competence measurement}
The desired gain control requires per-run loss on held-out text separate from the benchmark items. The DataDecide fields inspected in the source analysis don't supply it. The PolyPythias evaluation files considered there cover three sizes at early and final checkpoints, but contain accuracies, likelihood differences, and LAMBADA perplexity on the same benchmark items. That perplexity doesn't provide the required independent measurement scale.

The Mistral release of \citet{karamcheti2021} provides five GPT-2 Small and five GPT-2 Medium runs with about 600 checkpoints each, listed as 610 in the release summary, including WikiText-103 and OpenWebText validation perplexity. The source search didn't find the corresponding per-run benchmark battery. Without paired held-out loss and benchmark scores from the same runs, we can't use that release to identify the gain contribution.

\paragraph{Transport to PolyPythias}
The planned transport analysis scores nine runs per configuration on a nested 4,755-item battery. For three sizes, the source pipeline gives margin inflation of 1.40574 compared with 1.24395 for DataDecide, an excess ratio of 1.66323 computed from unrounded values. A scoring-kernel rewrite reproduces both inflation estimates to five decimals and reports the ratio as 1.66321, a difference that arises beyond the fifth decimal of the inputs. The configuration table requires the second-arm output rather than inspection of the DataDecide reduction alone.

On excess inflation, the DataDecide prediction falls approximately 40 percent below PolyPythias, while the raw inflation difference is approximately twelve percent. The planning document names aggregate standard deviation without resolving this scale choice. The excess-based failure assessment is therefore post hoc, and the raw inflation comparison falls inside the proposed twenty-percent band.

A later exploratory extension includes the 14M and 31M configurations, each with nine runs at step 143000 on the same 4,755 items. Within that rescore, margin inflation changes from 1.41372 at three sizes to 1.26199 at five sizes, while the excess ratio changes from 1.69592 to 1.07393. Accuracy changes from 2.28589 to 1.71085, compared with DataDecide's 1.07837. Only the three-size accuracy point estimate exceeds the stated detection threshold of approximately 2.05. The configuration-bootstrap interval for five-size accuracy runs from 0.869 to 2.428, and at three sizes its lower endpoint is undefined, with an upper endpoint of 2.971.

The margin configuration-bootstrap intervals are 0.939 to 1.781 at three sizes and 0.927 to 1.537 at five sizes. Both contain one and DataDecide's 1.24395, while the standard error falls from 0.190 to 0.150. These intervals limit conclusions about transport under either point-estimate assessment. The configuration bootstrap treats three or five configurations as enough to resample, so we also inverted the centred cluster test of Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary} with each configuration as its own cluster and $t(2)$ or $t(4)$ critical values. That construction gives margin sets of 0 to 2.106 at three sizes and 0.519 to 1.664 at five sizes, and it can't exclude any nonnegative accuracy value at either, so these data don't bound PolyPythias accuracy inflation at all. The rescore used one T4 with a 6,000-token cap instead of 30,000 tokens, and its three-size margin value of 1.41372 differs from the pipeline's 1.40574 by 0.6 percent. The excess-ratio difference is approximately two percent, with hardware and batching changed together. The three-versus-five comparison holds those conditions fixed within one job, and the source supplement describes 45 per-run score files from that job. For this revision we repeated the rescore on two T4 GPUs with the same scorer, token cap, items, and checkpoints. The repeat reproduces every estimate, configuration-bootstrap interval, and standard error that the earlier supplement reported, to the stated precision, and it corrects the five-size excess ratio, which the earlier supplement gave as 1.07395. We computed the inversion sets from the repeat's own score files, because the earlier supplement didn't report any inversion sets.

PolyPythias carries nine seeds for each configuration, which is enough to measure the ratio directly and to ask whether a three-run fit predicts it. The measurable quantity is the standard deviation of the battery mean over a set of runs, divided by the square root of the weighted sum of per-benchmark variances over the same runs, and it includes item noise. For each of the 84 ways of choosing three runs, we formed three predictions of that quantity on the six runs left out. Independence predicts one, the plug-in predicts the inflation estimate itself, and the decomposition adds each benchmark's estimated item noise to both sides of the ratio. On margins across three sizes the mean absolute log error is 0.339 for independence, 0.536 for the plug-in, and 0.512 for the decomposition, so neither fitted prediction beats the constant. The six-run target is itself noisy, because the measured margin ratio runs from 0.70 to 1.85 between its fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles, so this compares predictions of one noisy realisation rather than of the underlying ratio. Within that limit, the decomposition doesn't show out-of-sample value against a single configuration's ratio. Pooling the target across configurations changes the answer, which shows how much of the first comparison was target noise. When the six held-out runs give one ratio built from mean within-configuration variances, its standard deviation across splits falls to 0.140 on three-size margins. The five-size set gives 0.209 against 0.218 on margins and 0.251 against 0.364 on accuracy. The decomposition also beats the plug-in on every set, by between 0.010 and 0.105 of log error, so carrying item noise into the prediction helps on all four sets, where the covariance term helps on three of them and hurts on five-size margins. The 84 splits share runs and the four comparisons share configurations, so these are descriptive differences without an interval.

\section{Implementation details}

\subsection{Data streaming}
The implementation streams 122.9 GB of gzipped JSONL tarballs from the Hugging Face release. It retains the item identifier, correct-answer margin, and accuracy bit for the ten traits at the common checkpoint. Each run contains 37,682 items and approximately 30 MB of compressed output. Reduction of 375 runs produces approximately 48 MB of NumPy archives, with one array per run.

A frozen master seed generates a balanced permutation of item order within each trait. The split uses that permutation rather than a hash of item identifiers, and all runs within an arm reuse the same trait-specific assignment. The PolyPythias subsample therefore has its own split, rather than an item-hash split shared across arms. Regenerating the split requires the recorded item ordering and randomisation specification, without downloading the full tarballs.

\subsection{Interval calculation}
Derived quantities throughout the paper are computed from unrounded estimates, so recomputation from the printed three-decimal values can differ in the final digit.

We state the source calculation in terms of the squared ratio,
\[
\widehat\theta=\frac{\sum_cT_c}{\sum_cU_c},\qquad
\widehat\Lambda=\sqrt{\widehat\theta}.
\]
For each recipe $g$ among $G=25$, the linearised contribution and standard error are
\begin{equation}
\psi_g=\frac{\sum_{c\in g}(T_c-\widehat\theta U_c)}{\sum_cU_c},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=\left(\sum_{g=1}^{G}\psi_g^2\right)^{1/2}.
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
This standard error belongs to the squared-ratio scale used for studentisation. A reported standard error on the $\Lambda$ scale instead requires the corresponding delta-method conversion.

For draw $b$, independent recipe signs satisfy $v_{bg}\in\{-1,+1\}$ with equal probabilities. Every configuration takes the sign assigned to its recipe, and the pseudo-numerator becomes
\[
T_c^*=\widehat\theta U_c+v_{bg(c)}(T_c-\widehat\theta U_c).
\]
The denominator remains fixed at the observed $\sum_cU_c$. We recompute $\widehat\theta_b^*=\sum_cT_c^*/\sum_cU_c$ and the bootstrap standard error from the residuals $T_c^*-\widehat\theta_b^*U_c$. The bootstrap statistic is $t_b^*=(\widehat\theta_b^*-\widehat\theta)/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_b^*$, and the source calculation uses 4,999 draws with bootstrap seed zero.

Writing $q_\alpha$ for the empirical quantile of these statistics, the interval on $\theta$ is
\[
\left[\widehat\theta-q_{0.975}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}},
\widehat\theta-q_{0.025}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\right].
\]
Square-root transformation gives endpoints on the inflation scale when both endpoints are nonnegative. Monotonicity preserves the ordering of available endpoints, but it doesn't establish the original interval's coverage.

A draw with zero bootstrap standard error has no finite studentised statistic and is discarded. The routine requires at least $\max(100,0.9B)$ surviving draws before reporting an interval. A negative lower squared-ratio endpoint is recorded as missing rather than truncated, which explains the absent 1B margin lower endpoint in Table~\ref{tab:original7}. The configuration percentile bootstrap instead resamples all 125 configurations with replacement and recomputes both sums, while ignoring recipe membership.

The planning document specified wild cluster bootstrap-t inference with $t(16)$ critical values for seventeen estimation recipes. The realised analysis uses empirical bootstrap quantiles over all 25 recipes, following the failure to implement the planned split. The analysis record states that a $t(G-1)$ variant accompanies the bootstrap implementation. These alternatives differ in more than a change of cluster count.

\subsection{Practical considerations}
Within-configuration centring removes the shared performance mean before forming cross-half products. With only three runs, raw products would include a large contribution from mean competence rather than measuring covariance of deviations. The contrast representation in Appendix~\ref{app:centring} enforces that centring at the estimator input.

The permutation procedure reassigns run labels independently within each trait. Applying one joint permutation to all traits preserves cross-trait pairing and therefore doesn't generate this independence null. Identity permutations remain valid members of the randomisation distribution and shouldn't be excluded. For three runs, independent identities across all $K$ traits have probability $6^{-K}$ within one configuration.

Gzipped tarball members require sequential reading from their starts. The reduction therefore extracts the required fields on one pass, since an omitted field requires another complete stream. The default-versus-auxiliary step mismatch at 530M and 750M motivates the auxiliary-two-versus-auxiliary-three contrast, with checkpoint agreement checked separately for both auxiliary runs.

The pipeline lists the 375 checkpoint directories once, applies exponential backoff to rate limits, and caches the listing. Before estimation, it regenerates the common-step table from that cache and compares it with the initial version. This check can detect changes in the recorded step selection, but a cached listing alone can't detect new remote changes without a refreshed comparison.

Each of 27 jobs finished in under two minutes, with aggregate reported use below one card-hour rather than the budgeted six. Gold-token scoring materialises a float32 log-softmax tensor over a 50,304-token vocabulary. A 30,000-token batch requires approximately 6 GB for that tensor alone.

A configuration rescore fit on a 23 GB card at the planned token budget, while the 16 GB T4 checkpoint check used 6,000 tokens. Changing the budget changes batch grouping, which can affect floating-point results even when the mathematical scoring rule stays fixed. The transport rescore changed hardware and token budget together and shifted inflation by 0.6 percent. CPU handles the reduction and statistical analysis, with model scoring and calibration kernels as the reported exceptions.

\subsection{Release provenance}
The null checks examine mechanisms that can affect covariance estimates, but they don't independently establish the provenance of the three released runs. Differences in tokeniser builds, harness revisions, or other training conditions could contribute to the default-versus-auxiliary contrast if those conditions weren't held fixed. The auxiliary-run contrast also relies on documentation that the relevant settings match between its two runs.

The step table and disagreement checks expose some inconsistencies without replacing a release-side audit. Attribution of the observed contrasts to the documented seed changes therefore depends on the release documentation. This limitation applies independently of the estimator's behaviour on simulated or permuted data.

\subsection{Sensitivity to GPU architecture}
The primary Arm 2 scores use one card type. A separate Turing-versus-Ada comparison on one configuration reports mostly identical item scores, a mean absolute margin difference of $1.0\times10^{-3}$, and a largest difference of $3.5\times10^{-2}$. Relative to a margin standard deviation of 0.391, the mean difference has ratio $2.6\times10^{-3}$.

Eighteen of 4,755 accuracy bits change, or 0.38 percent. Their median absolute margin is $1.3\times10^{-3}$, and battery accuracy changes from 0.430074 to 0.430494, a difference of $4.2\times10^{-4}$. That difference is approximately eight percent of the reported accuracy seed standard deviation. This single comparison demonstrates sensitivity at that scale, but it doesn't establish a universal portability threshold for fp16 kernels.

Arm 1 uses released per-item outputs without recomputing forward passes, so this study doesn't add the same hardware variation to those scores. Shared hardware across Arm 2's 27 runs reduces one source of variation but doesn't independently validate accuracy across architectures.

\subsection{Centring through run contrasts}
\label{app:centring}
Raw cross-half products contain squared configuration means, which the analysis record reports can inflate the ratio by roughly three orders of magnitude. The described implementation projects scores onto an orthonormal basis of mean-zero run contrasts. Each basis row annihilates the all-ones vector, making raw and centred inputs equivalent under projection. Runtime checks test row sums and invariance of $\sum_c T_c$ under a shared configuration shift on both halves, addressing centring errors without testing measurement or exchangeability assumptions.
