\begin{abstract}
Papers often attach a run-to-run uncertainty to benchmark averages and build that uncertainty from each benchmark's variance alone, which ignores the covariance between scores, since a run that does well on one benchmark may do well on others. In simulation with margin-like covariance, this omission raises a comparison's false-positive rate from a nominal 0.05 to 0.113, and a correction estimated on the same battery returns it to 0.051. We estimate that correction with SNAP, a cross-half estimator that separates run-level variation from item-sampling noise, and apply it to 375 released DataDecide runs spanning 125 configurations and ten benchmarks. For per-byte margins, the standard deviation of the average is 1.244 times what independence predicts, with a nominal 95\% recipe-cluster interval of \ci{1.143}{1.338}. For accuracy the interval \ci{0.993}{1.157} includes one, which puts an upper limit of 1.157 on accuracy inflation in the primary analysis rather than showing independence. Both factors depend on which benchmarks are averaged, and without BoolQ they rise to 1.786 and 1.558. Because these ten benchmarks shaped the estimator, we registered a test on four unseen tasks. On all 225 runs at 530M, 750M, and 1B, held-out margin inflation is 1.217 with interval \ci{0.872}{1.498}, which fails the rule, while the original ten benchmarks on the same runs give 1.240 with interval \ci{1.054}{1.397} and pass it, a comparison fixed before any held-out score existed. The two point estimates differ by 0.024, the held-out interval runs 0.626 wide against 0.343, and the rule reads the lower limit. In simulation at that scope the rule passes in 0.926 of replicates at a true 1.244 under margin-like item noise and in 0.524 under accuracy-like noise. At a noise level we added afterwards, whose simulated median width of 0.640 sits close to the observed 0.626, it passes in 0.262, so the test couldn't separate transfer of the original covariance from its absence. Every analysis apart from that test is exploratory, and no registration carries an independently corroborated timestamp. On real recipe comparisons, the largest change a covariance-based standard error makes to the wrong-call rate is 0.009, with a recipe-bootstrap interval from $-0.002$ to 0.049, and we recommend paired replicate scores across the chosen battery with a benchmark-removal sensitivity check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A benchmark average can change across training runs even when the evaluation items stay fixed. For an equally weighted average of $K$ benchmarks, independent run deviations give variance $K^{-2}\operatorname{tr}\Sig$, where $\Sig$ is their cross-benchmark covariance matrix. Covariance adds the off-diagonal terms to that variance. In simulation with margin-like covariance, a comparison that assumes independence declares a difference in 0.113 of replicates with a true gap of zero, against a nominal rate of 0.05.

We estimate item-general covariance with two disjoint item halves for each benchmark. Within each configuration, we centre both half scores across replicate runs and then take their cross-half products, which under zero-mean item noise and conditional independence across halves estimate run covariance, including its diagonal. We adapt the established repeated-measurement construction to this setting and call it SNAP, Seed Noise Across Phenotypes, where phenotype names the score we call a trait, and we keep the name though the released runs differ in checkpoint step and training budget as well as seed.

Our empirical study uses DataDecide \citep{magnusson2025}, with 25 data recipes and five model sizes that each have three released replicates. Those 125 configurations supply 375 runs across ten benchmarks. The primary analysis reads released item scores and requires no model inference. We compare per-byte log-likelihood margins with accuracy on the same items. These scales respond differently to changes in confidence.

The margin inflation factor is 1.244 with recipe-cluster interval \ci{1.143}{1.338}, which excludes one, while accuracy gives 1.078 with interval \ci{0.993}{1.157}, which includes one and bounds accuracy inflation at 1.157 in the primary analysis (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).

That upper limit is fragile in two ways. The interval no longer includes one under five of the 25 recipe removals, under removal of the 750M band, and under removal of WinoGrande or BoolQ. Simulated nulls at a true ratio of one and at the observed 1.078 reach five or more such flips in only 0.018 and 0.032 of replicates. A simulation at the fitted accuracy cell excludes one in 0.111 of replicates against 0.970 on margins and would have detected a true ratio of 1.10 in only 0.135 of them. We therefore read the full-battery estimate as a bound and not as evidence of independence.

Because we built the estimator on these ten benchmarks, we registered a test on SciQ, MedMCQA, and multiple-choice DROP and CoQA, scored on the same checkpoints at 530M, 750M, and 1B, with a rule that required the lower interval limit for held-out margin inflation to exceed one. On all 225 runs the held-out estimate is 1.217 with interval \ci{0.872}{1.498}, so the registered test fails, while the original ten benchmarks on the same runs give 1.240 with interval \ci{1.054}{1.397} and pass it, a comparison fixed before any held-out score existed (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}). As an exploratory result, held-out accuracy inflation is 1.220 with interval \ci{1.066}{1.359}, which excludes one.

This paper makes four contributions to the measurement of run covariance. We define the item-general run covariance that cross-half products estimate and state which of its assumptions the released runs can't be shown to satisfy. We measure it on two score scales across the 375 released runs, with batch, gain, and format checks on its source, and margin inflation is 1.244 while the accuracy interval doesn't exclude one and only bounds accuracy inflation at 1.157 in the primary analysis. We register a test on unseen tasks and report it at its registered scope, where the held-out margin interval runs 0.626 wide against 0.343 for the original ten benchmarks on the same runs, and the rule reads the lower limit. We show what the covariance changes in practice. On real recipe comparisons at four sizes, with each pair left out of the estimates and checked against the ordering at 1B, a covariance-based standard error changes the wrong-call rate by at most 0.009. Neither the analysis plan nor the protocol's registration carries an independently corroborated timestamp, and the plan's screening split was never formed. Every analysis apart from the registered test is exploratory.

\section{Estimand and estimator}
\label{sec:estimator}
\subsection{Runs and score scales}
Let $c$ index configurations, $r$ index their $R$ replicate runs, and $j$ index benchmarks, with Table~\ref{tab:notation} in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} collecting the symbols of the cross-half construction and its empirical diagnostics. A configuration fixes a data recipe, model size, and nominal hyperparameters. In DataDecide the seed controls initialisation and data order together. Auxiliary training schedules introduce an additional source of run variation. For item $i$, define the per-byte margin and corresponding correctness indicator by
\begin{equation}
m_{crji}=\frac{\ell(\mathrm{gold}_i)}{\mathrm{bytes}(\mathrm{gold}_i)}-
\max_{v\ne\mathrm{gold}_i}\frac{\ell(v_i)}{\mathrm{bytes}(v_i)},
\qquad a_{crji}=\mathbf{1}\{m_{crji}>0\}.
\label{eq:scores}
\end{equation}
In Equation~\ref{eq:scores}, $\ell$ is the summed answer log-likelihood and $v$ ranges over alternatives, with a zero margin treated as incorrect. We average items within each benchmark, with MMLU macro-averaged over its 57 subjects, and denote the resulting margin and accuracy scores by $y^{\mathrm{marg}}$ and $y^{\mathrm{acc}}$, calling each benchmark-level score a trait.

For each half $H\in\{A,B\}$, our measurement model is
\begin{equation}
y^H_{crj}=\mu^H_{cj}+E_{crj}+\varepsilon^H_{crj}.
\end{equation}
A half-specific mean lets the two item sets differ in difficulty, the component $E_{crj}$ is the run deviation shared by the two item populations, while the term $\varepsilon^H_{crj}$ carries the remaining item-dependent variation. We assume independent, identically distributed run effects within a configuration when deriving unbiasedness, and write their covariance as $\Sigma_{E,c}$, although we can't sustain that interpretation in full, because training schedules differ within the actual population.

\subsection{Cross-half covariance}
We partition each benchmark's items into disjoint halves with a frozen random seed and use the same assignment for its replicate runs. We centre each half score across the replicate runs, defining $d^H_{crj}=y^H_{crj}-R^{-1}\sum_s y^H_{csj}$. If the item errors have conditional mean zero and zero covariance between the two halves across the relevant runs and benchmarks, then
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[d^A_{crj}d^B_{crk}]=(1-1/R)\Sigma_{E,c}(j,k).
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:identity} covers diagonal entries without requiring Gaussian run effects. Disjoint item identifiers alone don't establish the required independence, since passages, duplicate content, and other shared item effects can connect the halves, a risk we check for the one benchmark with documented shared passages by assigning whole passages to halves in Section~\ref{sec:composition}. Item noise shared across benchmarks and repeated across halves would also enter the numerator. Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} shows that a 0.124 share of item variance would reproduce 1.244, so the estimate is identified only up to that component.

We write the average centred score as $g^H_{cr}=K^{-1}\sum_j d^H_{crj}$ and compute
\begin{equation}
T_c=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_r g^A_{cr}g^B_{cr},\qquad
U_c=\frac{1}{K^2(R-1)}\sum_{j,r}d^A_{crj}d^B_{crj}.
\label{eq:tu}
\end{equation}
The respective expectations of the two statistics in Equation~\ref{eq:tu} are $K^{-2}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{E,c}\mathbf{1}$ and $K^{-2}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{E,c}$. We sum each statistic across configurations before dividing, which gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat\Lam=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_c T_c}{\sum_c U_c}},\qquad
\Lam^2=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sig\mathbf{1}}{\operatorname{tr}\Sig}
=1+(K-1)\bar r_E,\qquad K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{K}{\Lam^2},
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\Sig=N^{-1}\sum_c\Sigma_{E,c}$ is the covariance matrix averaged across configurations. We express the off-diagonal contribution through the effective coefficient
\begin{equation}
\bar r_E=\frac{\sum_{j\ne k}\Sigma_E(j,k)}{(K-1)\operatorname{tr}\Sig}.
\label{eq:effective}
\end{equation}
This effective covariance coefficient equals the mean off-diagonal correlation when the marginal variances are equal, and with unequal variances it differs from a normalised weighted mean correlation (Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}). $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ compares this battery against independent benchmarks of the same average variance, and it doesn't count independent information sources.

Unbiased estimates of $T_c$ and $U_c$ don't make their ratio or square root unbiased, and either pooled sum can be nonpositive in a finite sample. We use calibration simulations to check finite-sample behaviour in specified populations.

\subsection{Resolution and inference}
For an infinitesimal additive shift in Gaussian item margins, the ratio of accuracy to margin noise per unit squared sensitivity is
\begin{equation}
\frac{p(1-p)}{\phi(z_0)^2},\qquad z_0=\Phi^{-1}(1-p).
\label{eq:information}
\end{equation}
At an illustrative accuracy of $p=0.35$, this model gives a ratio of about 1.66. Its minimum is $\pi/2$, which means the planned lower threshold of 1.4 for per-trait ratios (prediction eight of the plan, Appendix~\ref{app:design}) couldn't falsify this model. This calculation describes only the assumed location-shift model and doesn't show an empirical information advantage for arbitrary margin distributions.

We report nominal 95\% wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ intervals over the 25 recipe clusters, using 4,999 Rademacher draws and a linearisation of the squared ratio, with the implementation, including its fixed observed denominator during resampling, stated in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}. We also report a configuration percentile bootstrap, which resamples both sums but ignores recipe dependence, and neither interval width alone identifies a design effect.

A simulation with 2,000 replicates per population and the same 4,999 draws gives wild bootstrap coverage between 0.928 and 0.954 across the twelve populations that Table~\ref{tab:coverage} in the appendix lists. The band-shared populations in the discussion fall lower. We report the wild interval because the configuration percentile bootstrap drops to 0.872 under recipe-shared effects and a recipe percentile bootstrap covers 0.857 to 0.940. Cross-half error correlation of 0.1 biases the estimate downward by 0.014 at margin-like noise and 0.016 at accuracy-like noise, so this channel of item dependence understates inflation. Under accuracy-like item noise the null population carries a downward bias of 0.011, so finite-sample bias understates an accuracy excess rather than manufacturing one. A cluster test-inversion interval that we added later covers between 0.935 and 0.956 in a 10,000-replicate repeat of the same populations and gives \ci{1.095}{1.323} for margins and \ci{0.995}{1.162} for accuracy on the observed data, which moves the margin lower endpoint down by 0.048. A residual-scaled wild interval covers 0.943 to 0.958 in the same populations at 4,000 replicates and moves no conclusion, and since we chose it after seeing four of the cells we report the shipped interval throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

\section{Data and analysis scope}
DataDecide supplies the ten benchmarks ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, BoolQ, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, MMLU, OpenBookQA, PIQA, Social IQa, and WinoGrande. We analyse 37,682 items per run across 25 recipes at 150M, 300M, 530M, 750M, and 1B parameters, since smaller released models lack the required per-item replicate coverage. Replicate seed labels are $\{2,14,15\}$ below 1B and $\{2,4,5\}$ at 1B.

In each configuration we select the largest checkpoint step available for all three runs. An index of the release's checkpoint files shows that this step scores the 750M default runs at an average of 41.5\% of their final step, with a range of 18\% to 44\%, and the 530M default runs at 87\%, with a range of 39\% to 89\%. In 26 configurations, one at 530M and 25 at 750M, the largest final step exceeds the smallest by more than a factor of 1.5. DataDecide stops the auxiliary replicates below 1B at 25 percent of the 1B compute budget. We interpret the full estimate as covariance among the released runs at the selected steps, and we report an auxiliary-run contrast that removes the default-versus-auxiliary batch component. At 750M the default runs stop well short of their released final models, although the auxiliary runs sit close to their own final steps in every 750M configuration but one. When we drop the 26 severely truncated configurations, which removes the 750M band, margin inflation is 1.276 with interval \ci{1.114}{1.420} and accuracy inflation is 1.120 with interval \ci{1.020}{1.208}, a subset we chose after seeing the full-sample estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:schedule} finds no detectable rank correlation between a configuration's influence on the estimate and its truncation. At the adjacent earlier shared step the accuracy interval is \ci{1.044}{1.210} and excludes one (Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoint}), which means the accuracy bound depends on the scored checkpoint as well. That check can't see an offset shared by every configuration, and the auxiliary contrast doesn't remove differences shared with the checkpoint schedules.

The analysis plan assigned eight recipes to screening and seventeen to estimation, but we used all recipes without drawing that partition. Every analysis choice drew on all 125 configurations. The plan also has neither a supplied original file nor an independently corroborated date. An enumeration over the $\binom{25}{17}=1{,}081{,}575$ possible estimation sets produces margin estimates from 1.037 to 1.317 and accuracy estimates from 0.963 to 1.191, none of which reaches the planned thresholds of 1.349 and 1.40, but this retrospective calculation can't restore a holdout or undo choices made after viewing the data.

We retain the full ten-benchmark battery for the primary estimand, including benchmarks with negative estimated diagonal covariance, since an unbiased moment estimate can be negative near zero. If we deleted a benchmark because we saw its negative estimate, we would change both the selection rule and the estimand, and the standardised diagnostics inherit that problem because they keep only traits with positive variance.

We use PolyPythias \citep{vanderwal2025} for an exploratory transfer check, scoring nine seeds at each of 70M, 160M, and 410M on 4,755 nested items, although its three configurations can't support a precise population comparison.

\section{Sensitivity to batch and score scale}
Default and auxiliary runs come from different batches. We decompose within-configuration contrasts into a default-versus-auxiliary component and an auxiliary-versus-auxiliary component. The latter removes an offset shared by both auxiliary runs and retains one contrast per configuration, but it doesn't confirm matching software provenance or remove differences shared with the selected checkpoint schedules. A simulated batch offset provides a separate sensitivity check.

A positive multiplicative gain changes margin magnitudes while preserving their signs. That leaves threshold accuracy unchanged by construction on every item. We examine this mechanism with a gain covariate $s_{cr}$, the mean per-byte answer log-likelihood, and with simulations that vary the gain scale and margin distribution, alongside a leave-one-benchmark-out competence proxy $x_{cr}^{(-j)}$ fitted on the opposite item half, using
\begin{equation}
y_{crj}=\alpha_{cj}+\beta_jx_{cr}^{(-j)}+\gamma_js_{cr}+u_{crj}.
\end{equation}
Each benchmark has a slope on each covariate over 250 run contrasts. We treat these adjustments as descriptive and give none of them a mechanistic reading. The gain covariate shares item noise with the outcomes, and the competence proxy averages the same seed deviations it adjusts, which removes shared covariance by construction. Appendix~\ref{app:proxy} shows that matched in-sample simulations with the observed seed covariance return 0.745 to 0.889 against the observed repaired margin adjustments of 0.731 to 0.890.

The per-benchmark diagnostics that follow don't enter the headline ratio, which uses covariance products directly, but they show how little seed signal accuracy carries. Per-benchmark reliability averages 0.652 on margins and 0.324 on accuracy. Seven accuracy benchmarks and three margin benchmarks have reliability below 0.50, WinoGrande has a negative estimated variance on margins, and PIQA and WinoGrande have negative accuracy estimates. The accuracy correlation estimate also contains an entry of 1.296 and an eigenvalue of $-0.55$, which makes it an invalid correlation matrix without further regularisation.

When we remove BoolQ, margin inflation rises to 1.786 with interval \ci{1.689}{1.877}, and accuracy inflation rises to 1.558 with interval \ci{1.412}{1.687}, while the removal of CommonsenseQA instead gives inflation estimates of 1.096 and 1.014. BoolQ contributes 68\% of the margin covariance trace and 84\% of the accuracy trace. The removal estimates imply that the off-diagonal terms involving BoolQ are negative, and they sum to about $-0.15$ of the margin trace and $-0.06$ of the accuracy trace, which is why its removal lowers the denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:lambda} by more than the numerator. The same removal lowers the aggregate standard deviation on both scales. A larger factor thus accompanies a less variable average. The factor compares each battery with independence at that battery's own marginal variances. Lowering BoolQ's weight from a tenth to 0.0867 already stops the accuracy interval including one (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}). We recovered the 2,938 passages behind BoolQ's 3,270 questions from the release's request files and assigned each passage whole to one half, a split that changes the two estimates by $-0.0002$ and $-0.0001$ on average across fifty re-splits. The BoolQ diagonal moves by 0.03\% and 0.15\% (Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}). Shared passages don't account for its trace share, although that check can't reach other shared item effects. A permutation that reshuffles each run's item deviations inside every group leaves all trait scores fixed and breaks the tie between particular items and particular runs, and across 2,000 such permutations 0.087 reach or exceed the observed margin estimate and 0.352 the observed accuracy estimate. Item-level sharing accounts for about 0.001 of the margin estimate and 0.002 of the accuracy estimate, although an effect that every item of a benchmark carries survives this permutation untouched.

Recipe removal moves margin inflation between 1.207 and 1.256, and size removal moves it between 1.178 and 1.289, where the deletion of 530M gives \ci{0.993}{1.338} and stops excluding one, while the corresponding accuracy ranges run from 1.065 to 1.099 and from 1.047 to 1.115. Benchmark-removal sensitivity is larger, and among equal-weight subsets of the same size those containing BoolQ average 0.115 lower margin inflation at two benchmarks and 0.596 lower at nine than those without it. Our design treats this battery as fixed, and that spread isn't a distribution over benchmark collections. A sweep of 2,000 weightings from a flat Dirichlet leaves 0.817 of margin intervals excluding one. Appendix~\ref{app:composition} separates benchmark count, identity, and weights.

\subsection{Format structure and mechanism checks}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/covariance.pdf}

The accuracy estimate has a negative eigenvalue, and its spectrum is a diagnostic of the moment estimate rather than a valid population correlation spectrum. Among the nine defined margin traits, mean estimated correlations are 0.624 within scoring formats and 0.007 across formats. Figure~\ref{fig:covariance} shows the full correlation estimates with their eigenvalue spectra. Each benchmark appears in exactly one scoring format. With that design we can't separate format from task content causally. If we retain only cross-format off-diagonal covariance, the diagnostic inflation is 0.921 with interval \ci{0.801}{1.035}, compared with 1.244 for the full matrix, although this diagnostic isn't the variance of a separately observed aggregate, since a masked covariance matrix doesn't have to stay positive semidefinite. The accuracy diagnostic is 0.965 with interval \ci{0.848}{1.063}. Without BoolQ the cross-format margin diagnostic is 1.006, compared with 1.786 without the restriction.

We also transformed the saved margins item by item, holding items and runs fixed, to test whether the scoring rule alone can produce the gap between the two scales. Division of each benchmark's margins by their pooled standard deviation gives margin inflation of 1.182 with interval \ci{1.094}{1.265}, a hyperbolic-tangent bound gives 1.141 with interval \ci{1.053}{1.222}, and their signs alone give 1.079 with interval \ci{0.992}{1.158}, which includes one (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}). These are interventions on the scoring rule and don't show how training produces large margins.

The auxiliary contrast gives margin inflation of 1.256 and accuracy inflation of 1.084, so the point estimates hold, although the accuracy interval includes one and we haven't corrected the full-set estimate for that exposure. The planned signature, a collapse of margin inflation after gain adjustment, didn't appear, although the proxy limitations restrict any interpretation of that failure, since gain adjustment raises margin inflation to 1.275 and accuracy inflation to 1.097 (Table~\ref{tab:primary}), with the joint and competence-only adjustments in Appendix~\ref{app:proxy}. The shipped competence proxy is also degenerate, since its variance floor lets WinoGrande supply more than 99.99\% of the squared standardised inputs to the margin proxy (Appendix~\ref{app:proxy}).

At a gain standard deviation of 0.05, the default gain simulation produces margin inflation of 1.148, about 61\% of the observed excess above one, whereas at the estimated gain standard deviation of 0.042 the default and distribution-matched simulators produce about 44\% and 6\% of that excess. The recipe bootstrap interval for that standard deviation, 0.011 to 0.063, moves the default share from 2\% to 91\%. The simulations therefore provide no identified causal fraction for sharpness. Under a batch-offset simulation with offset standard deviation 0.05, the factors are 1.004 and 1.003, about 1.5\% of the observed margin excess.

\subsection{Calibration and external checks}
Under independent noise the simulation gives mean inflation of 1.0009 on margins and 1.0004 on accuracy, and the recovery slope across effective covariance coefficients from zero to 0.50 is 0.998, while fifty item re-splits on the observed data give standard deviations of 0.002 and 0.008. These checks assess selected reference distributions and can't rule out training-schedule confounding.

The 500-permutation accuracy tail fraction is 0.028, and a later run with 1,000 permutations gives 0.021. Both reach one in twenty while the primary cluster interval contains one. We retain both results since the two procedures condition on different aspects of the design.

PolyPythias gives margin inflation of 1.406 on the three planned sizes, compared with 1.244 for DataDecide. A five-size rescore gives margin inflation of 1.262 with interval \ci{0.927}{1.537} and accuracy inflation of 1.711 with interval \ci{0.869}{2.428}, where test inversion leaves accuracy unbounded, and these data therefore don't bound PolyPythias accuracy inflation (Appendix~\ref{app:transport}). Released single-configuration panels from \citet{heineman2025} give likelihood inflation of 1.705 and 1.625 on eighteen traits and accuracy inflation of 0.989 and 0.868 on eight traits under initialisation and data-order seeds. Without cross-half correction, intervals, or a matched trait count, they suggest the same score-scale difference without confirming the DataDecide estimand.

\subsection{Predicting aggregate uncertainty}
\label{sec:prediction}
We compared five covariance models and an exploratory rescaled plug-in on five recipe folds left out of fitting, giving each the held-out marginal seed standard deviations (Table~\ref{tab:prediction}). On margins the rank-one, rank-one plus gain, and full models lower the mean squared error of log aggregate standard deviation by about 0.03 from the 0.0358 of independence, with paired recipe-bootstrap intervals that exclude zero, although the rank-one interval reaches zero when nonpositive held-out variances are filled from training folds. On accuracy every such interval includes zero, and no model separates from independence in an operational comparison that refits without held-out marginals.

Under independent Gaussian run errors with a common aggregate standard deviation, a true score gap $\Delta$ reverses sign with probability
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{flip}}(\Delta)=\Phi\left(-\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{agg}}}\right).
\end{equation}
The reported accuracy deviation of 0.00514 gives probabilities of about 0.246 for a half-point gap and 0.085 for a one-point gap. Under independence, the same gaps give about 0.229 and 0.069. Of 1,500 observed same-size gaps, the reported census places 455 below the corresponding 5\% risk threshold, though noisy gaps give no lower bound on the fraction of small true gaps (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}).

We checked the same question on real recipe comparisons. At each size below 1B we called a pair of recipes when their aggregate gap exceeded two standard errors, computed either under independence or with the estimated covariance, with the pair left out of both estimates. We scored each call against the pair's ordering at 1B whenever that ordering itself cleared two corrected standard errors. Across the four sizes and both scales, the correction removes between 1 and 12 of the 173 to 223 calls on such pairs, which changes the share of wrong calls by at most 0.009, from rates between 0.029 and 0.070. Every recipe-bootstrap interval for that change reaches zero (Table~\ref{tab:decision}). A wrong call here also counts genuine rank changes between sizes. These rates therefore aren't false-positive rates in the usual sense.

\section{Related work}
\citet{bouthillier2021} derive variance inflation from correlated repeated trainings of one pipeline on one task and measure the variance from each source in five case studies, whereas we apply covariance accounting across benchmarks within a replicate run. \citet{reimers2017}, \citet{zhou2020}, and \citet{madaan2024} study seed-related evaluation variability, and \citet{dodge2020} examine whether favourable initialisations transfer across tasks. \citet{jordan2024} separates example-specific training variance from distribution-level variation and compares accuracies across related evaluation sets. Among the studies we located, none estimates the covariance of run noise between benchmarks or an effective benchmark count derived from that covariance.

The cross-half construction follows repeated-measurement and variance-component ideas from \citet{spearman1904}, \citet{cronbach1951}, and \citet{searle1992}. The quantitative-genetics treatment of covariance structure \citep{martin1977} is an analogy that our model doesn't carry as far as separate variance components, while \citet{cheverud1988} motivates the phenotypic plug-in comparison in Table~\ref{tab:prediction}, which fails here on the transfer of correlation structure.

\citet{heineman2025} measure per-benchmark signal and noise on DataDecide and OLMo checkpoints and relate the ratio to decision accuracy, but don't estimate covariance between benchmarks. Few-cluster inference follows the concerns studied by \citet{cameron2008} and \citet{mackinnon2017}. \citet{cheverud2001}, \citet{nyholt2004}, and \citet{li2005} count effective independent tests to adjust thresholds, whereas we estimate the run covariance of a fixed battery (Appendix~\ref{app:design}).

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The full-battery accuracy result gives an upper bound of 1.157 in the primary analysis, which the earlier-step and truncation checks exceed at 1.210 and 1.208, and no evidence of independence. Removing BoolQ raises the accuracy estimate from 1.078 to 1.558, with an interval of 1.412 to 1.687. On margins, the covariance sits almost entirely within scoring formats. The cross-format entries alone give a diagnostic of 0.921.

Apart from the registered test, which failed at its registered scope, the empirical study remains exploratory. The passage-aware split moves both estimates within re-split variation, and that null follows from BoolQ's passage structure rather than from a sensitive test. A scalar item effect shared across benchmarks and repeated across halves would enter the numerator whole, and shares of 0.124 and 0.009 of item-noise variance reproduce 1.244 and 1.078, a component we haven't measured and can't separate from seed covariance with the diagnostics we ran (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). We make no claim about that mechanism, since repaired margin proxies fall below one even in simulations without a separate competence variable, apart from the clipped-drop weighting at 0.889 against a simulated fifth percentile of 0.908.

We cluster on recipes, but the release shares one seed label across every recipe inside a size band. A run effect following the band would break that choice and take simulated coverage to 0.821 at a quarter of latent variance and 0.580 at a half. A permutation test that aligns runs by batch slot bounds that effect at 0.044 of the seed covariance on margins, where simulated coverage is 0.941 at a share of 0.05, although that test's power is only 0.490 at the bound. Clusters on the five size bands leave both conclusions standing. Within a single size the evidence is weaker, as the cluster-$t$ 1B margin interval of 0.811 to 1.628 includes one (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}).

For practice we recommend paired replicate scores across the chosen battery, which give the aggregate seed standard deviation directly, and a benchmark-removal sensitivity check. A scaled independence standard error, with a ratio estimated on the same population, returns the simulated false-positive rate from 0.113 to 0.051, but carried to a battery whose own ratio is 1.5, the same scaling leaves the error at 0.101. Under an equicorrelated seed covariance, three runs per configuration let the margin interval exclude one in 0.997 of simulated populations, while accuracy needs eight runs to reach 0.803.
