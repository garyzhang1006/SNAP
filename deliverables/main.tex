\begin{abstract}
Papers often attach a run-to-run uncertainty to benchmark averages. They build it from each benchmark's variance alone, which ignores the covariance between scores, since a run that does well on one benchmark may do well on others. We estimate that covariance with SNAP, a cross-half estimator that separates run-level variation from the noise of sampling items under conditional independence assumptions. We apply it to 375 released DataDecide runs spanning 125 configurations and ten benchmarks. For per-byte margins, the standard deviation of the average is 1.244 times what independence predicts, with a nominal 95\% recipe-cluster interval of \ci{1.143}{1.338}. The accuracy factor is smaller, at 1.078 \ci{0.993}{1.157}, and its interval includes one. Simulated coverage of these intervals lies between 0.928 and 0.954 across twelve populations. Both factors depend on which benchmarks are averaged, and removing BoolQ raises them to 1.786 and 1.558. Because these ten benchmarks shaped the estimator, we registered a test on four unseen tasks scored on the same checkpoints. We could afford to score only the 530M runs before submission, so we report the test as modified to that size, where held-out margin inflation is 1.198 \ci{0.864}{1.454} and the rule fails. A third of four-benchmark subsets from the original battery also fail it at that size. In simulation, ignoring the covariance raises the false-positive rate of a margin comparison from a nominal 0.05 to 0.113. On real recipe comparisons checked against the 1B ranking, however, a covariance-based standard error changes the rate of wrong calls by at most 0.009. Every analysis apart from the registered test is exploratory, and we recommend reporting replicate uncertainty with a benchmark-removal sensitivity check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A benchmark average can change across training runs even when the evaluation items stay fixed. For an equally weighted average of $K$ benchmarks, independent run deviations give variance $K^{-2}\operatorname{tr}\Sig$, where $\Sig$ is their cross-benchmark covariance matrix. Covariance adds the off-diagonal terms to that variance, so a collection of marginal standard deviations alone doesn't determine the uncertainty of the average. Researchers need paired benchmark scores from replicate runs to estimate that contribution.

We estimate item-general covariance with two disjoint item halves for each benchmark. Within each configuration, we centre both half scores across replicate runs, then take their cross-half products. Under zero-mean item noise and conditional independence across halves, these products estimate run covariance, including its diagonal. We adapt the established repeated-measurement construction to this setting, and call it SNAP, Seed Noise Across Phenotypes, borrowing phenotype from quantitative genetics for the score we call a trait.

Our empirical study uses DataDecide \citep{magnusson2025}, with 25 data recipes and five model sizes that each have three released replicates. Those 125 configurations supply 375 runs across ten benchmarks, and we read released item scores, so the primary analysis requires no model inference. We compare per-byte log-likelihood margins with accuracy on the same items, because these scales respond differently to changes in confidence.

Training conditions vary across the released runs in this population. Auxiliary replicates use different training budgets below 1B parameters, and 92 configurations have mismatched final checkpoint steps. We compare the largest step shared by all three runs, but this rule doesn't align their training schedules. We therefore interpret the full estimate as covariance among the released runs at the selected steps, and report an auxiliary-run contrast that removes the default-versus-auxiliary batch component.

Margin scores carry clear covariance, with an inflation factor of 1.244 whose recipe-cluster interval excludes one. The accuracy estimate of 1.078 sits close to independence for the full battery, but that reading is fragile. BoolQ contributes 84\% of the estimated accuracy variance trace, and removing it raises accuracy inflation to 1.558. On the 123 configurations with an earlier shared checkpoint, scoring that checkpoint moves accuracy inflation from 1.082 to 1.130, with an interval that excludes one. The paired interval for that change includes zero, as Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoint} reports. Deleting parts of the design moves the accuracy conclusion in one direction only, since the interval stops including one under five of the 25 recipe removals. A null sweep reaches five or more such flips in 0.018 of replicates. The design also lacks the power to resolve an effect of that size, because a simulation at the fitted accuracy cell excludes one in 0.111 of replicates against 0.970 on margins. We therefore don't read the small full-battery estimate as evidence of independence.

We built the estimator while looking at these ten benchmarks, so we registered a test on four tasks outside them. They are SciQ, MedMCQA, and multiple-choice versions of DROP and CoQA, scored on the same checkpoints at 530M, 750M, and 1B. The rule required the lower interval limit for held-out margin inflation to exceed one. Only the 530M runs finished scoring before submission, and on them the held-out estimate is 1.198 with interval \ci{0.864}{1.454}, so the test as modified to that size fails. Four-benchmark subsets of the original battery at 530M have a median of 1.244, and a third of them fail the same rule, so the test couldn't separate transfer of the original covariance from its absence. Held-out accuracy inflation is 1.373, and its interval excludes one.

We define the item-general run covariance that cross-half products estimate, state when released runs satisfy its assumptions, and measure it on two score scales, with batch, gain, and format checks on its source. In simulation, ignoring the covariance raises a margin comparison's false-positive rate from a nominal 0.05 to 0.113, and a measured scale correction returns it to 0.051. On real recipe comparisons at four sizes, checked against the ordering at 1B, a covariance-based standard error changes the wrong-call rate by at most 0.009. An earlier analysis plan specified a screening split that we never formed and carries no independent timestamp, so every analysis apart from the registered test is exploratory.

\section{Estimand and estimator}
\label{sec:estimator}
\subsection{Runs and score scales}
Let $c$ index configurations, $r$ index their $R$ replicate runs, and $j$ index benchmarks, with Table~\ref{tab:notation} in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} collecting every symbol used below. A configuration fixes a data recipe, model size, and nominal hyperparameters. In DataDecide, the seed controls initialisation and data order together, and auxiliary training schedules introduce an additional source of run variation. For item $i$, define the per-byte margin and corresponding correctness indicator by
\begin{equation}
m_{crji}=\frac{\ell(\mathrm{gold}_i)}{\mathrm{bytes}(\mathrm{gold}_i)}-
\max_{v\ne\mathrm{gold}_i}\frac{\ell(v_i)}{\mathrm{bytes}(v_i)},
\qquad a_{crji}=\mathbf{1}\{m_{crji}>0\}.
\label{eq:scores}
\end{equation}
Here $\ell$ is the summed answer log-likelihood, $v$ ranges over alternatives, and a zero margin is treated as incorrect. We average items within each benchmark, with MMLU macro-averaged over its 57 subjects, and denote the resulting margin and accuracy scores by $y^{\mathrm{marg}}$ and $y^{\mathrm{acc}}$, calling each benchmark-level score a trait.

For each half $H\in\{A,B\}$, our measurement model is
\begin{equation}
y^H_{crj}=\mu^H_{cj}+E_{crj}+\varepsilon^H_{crj}.
\end{equation}
A half-specific mean lets the two item sets differ in difficulty. The component $E_{crj}$ is the run deviation shared by the two item populations, while the term $\varepsilon^H_{crj}$ carries the remaining item-dependent variation. We assume independent, identically distributed run effects within a configuration when deriving unbiasedness, and write their covariance as $\Sigma_{E,c}$. We can't sustain that interpretation in full, because training schedules differ within the actual population.

\subsection{Cross-half covariance}
Each benchmark's items are partitioned into disjoint halves with a frozen random seed, and the same assignment is used for its replicate runs. We centre each half score across the replicate runs, defining $d^H_{crj}=y^H_{crj}-R^{-1}\sum_s y^H_{csj}$. If the item errors have conditional mean zero and zero covariance between the two halves across the relevant runs and benchmarks, then
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[d^A_{crj}d^B_{crk}]=(1-1/R)\Sigma_{E,c}(j,k).
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
This identity covers diagonal entries, and it doesn't require Gaussian run effects. Disjoint item identifiers alone don't establish the required independence, because passages, duplicate content, and other shared item effects can connect the halves. We check the one benchmark with documented shared passages by assigning whole passages to halves in Section~\ref{sec:composition}.

We write the average centred score as $g^H_{cr}=K^{-1}\sum_j d^H_{crj}$ and compute
\begin{equation}
T_c=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_r g^A_{cr}g^B_{cr},\qquad
U_c=\frac{1}{K^2(R-1)}\sum_{j,r}d^A_{crj}d^B_{crj}.
\label{eq:tu}
\end{equation}
The respective expectations of these two statistics are $K^{-2}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{E,c}\mathbf{1}$ and $K^{-2}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{E,c}$. We sum each statistic across configurations before dividing, which gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat\Lam=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_c T_c}{\sum_c U_c}},\qquad
\Lam^2=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sig\mathbf{1}}{\operatorname{tr}\Sig}
=1+(K-1)\bar r_E,\qquad K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{K}{\Lam^2},
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\Sig=N^{-1}\sum_c\Sigma_{E,c}$ is the covariance matrix averaged across configurations. We express the off-diagonal contribution through the effective coefficient
\begin{equation}
\bar r_E=\frac{\sum_{j\ne k}\Sigma_E(j,k)}{(K-1)\operatorname{tr}\Sig}.
\label{eq:effective}
\end{equation}
This effective covariance coefficient equals the mean off-diagonal correlation when the marginal variances are equal. With unequal variances, it differs from a normalised weighted mean correlation, as Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} explains. At the same root-mean-square marginal deviation, the effective count compares the battery with independent benchmarks, and it isn't a count of independent information sources.

Unbiased estimates of $T_c$ and $U_c$ don't make their ratio or square root unbiased, and either pooled sum can be nonpositive in a finite sample. We use calibration simulations to check finite-sample behaviour in specified populations, where the ratio and square-root transformations can introduce bias.

\subsection{Resolution and inference}
For an infinitesimal additive shift in Gaussian item margins, the ratio of accuracy to margin noise per unit squared sensitivity is
\begin{equation}
\frac{p(1-p)}{\phi(z_0)^2},\qquad z_0=\Phi^{-1}(1-p).
\label{eq:information}
\end{equation}
At an illustrative accuracy of $p=0.35$, this model gives a ratio of about 1.66. Its minimum is $\pi/2$, which means the planned lower threshold of 1.4 couldn't falsify this model. This calculation describes only the assumed location-shift model, and it doesn't show an empirical information advantage for arbitrary margin distributions. The aggregate reliability calculation uses $\rho_g=\Lam^2/(\Lam^2+\bar v)$, with $\bar v$ the item-noise contribution on that scale.

We report nominal 95\% wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ intervals over the 25 recipe clusters, using 4,999 Rademacher draws and a linearisation of the squared ratio. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} states the implementation, including its fixed observed denominator during resampling. We also report a configuration percentile bootstrap, which resamples both sums but ignores recipe dependence, so the two procedures make different assumptions, and neither interval width alone identifies a design effect.

A simulation with 2,000 replicates per population and the same 4,999 draws gives wild bootstrap coverage between 0.928 and 0.954 across twelve populations. Coverage holds between 0.942 and 0.952 as the runs per configuration move from two to ten, so the three the release ships aren't the binding constraint. Table~\ref{tab:coverage} in the appendix lists all twelve populations and their constructions. We report the wild interval because the configuration percentile bootstrap drops to 0.872 under recipe-shared effects and a recipe percentile bootstrap covers 0.857 to 0.940. Cross-half error correlation of 0.1 biases the estimate downward by about 0.015, which places residual item dependence on the side of understating inflation. Under accuracy-like item noise, the null population gives a mean estimate of 0.989, a downward bias of 0.011, and the population at 1.078 shows a bias of $-0.007$, so finite-sample bias understates rather than manufactures an accuracy excess. A cluster test-inversion interval that we added later covers between 0.935 and 0.956 in the same populations, and on the observed data it gives \ci{1.095}{1.323} for margins and \ci{0.995}{1.162} for accuracy, so it moves the margin lower endpoint down by 0.048.

\section{Data and analysis scope}
DataDecide supplies the ten benchmarks ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, BoolQ, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, MMLU, OpenBookQA, PIQA, Social IQa, and WinoGrande. We analyse 37,682 items per run across 25 recipes at 150M, 300M, 530M, 750M, and 1B parameters, because smaller released models lack the required per-item replicate coverage. Replicate seed labels are $\{2,14,15\}$ below 1B and $\{2,4,5\}$ at 1B.

In each configuration, the largest checkpoint step available for all three runs is selected. In 26 configurations, one at 530M and 25 at 750M, the largest final step exceeds the smallest by more than a factor of 1.5, and the shared step then truncates the longer runs. An index of the release's checkpoint files shows that the selected step averages 87\% of the default run's final step at 530M, with a range of 39\% to 89\%, and 41\% at 750M, with a range of 18\% to 44\%. At 750M the default runs therefore stop well short of their released final models, although the auxiliary runs sit close to their own final steps. Dropping the 26 severely truncated configurations gives margin inflation of 1.276 with interval \ci{1.114}{1.420}, and a configuration's influence on the estimate shows no detectable rank correlation with its truncation, as Appendix~\ref{app:schedule} reports.

The analysis plan assigned eight recipes to screening and seventeen to estimation, but we used all recipes without drawing that partition. As a result, analysis choices used all 125 configurations, and the plan has neither a supplied original file nor an independently corroborated date. We report its thresholds as historical targets because their timing remains unverified. An enumeration over the $\binom{25}{17}=1{,}081{,}575$ possible estimation sets produces margin estimates from 1.037 to 1.317 and accuracy estimates from 0.963 to 1.191. None reaches the planned thresholds of 1.349 and 1.40, but this retrospective calculation can't restore a holdout or account for choices made after viewing the data.

We retain the full ten-benchmark battery for the primary estimand, including benchmarks with negative estimated diagonal covariance. An unbiased moment estimate can be negative near zero, and deleting a benchmark after observing that sign changes both selection and the estimand. Standardised diagnostics, which use only defined positive-variance traits, carry this selection limitation.

We use PolyPythias \citep{vanderwal2025} for an exploratory transfer check, scoring nine seeds at each of 70M, 160M, and 410M on 4,755 nested items, and its three configurations can't support a precise population comparison. We also examine released run-level scores from \citet{heineman2025}, whose single-configuration panels lack item-level outputs for the cross-half correction. A later five-size PolyPythias analysis adds 14M and 31M under a different scoring batch budget.

\section{Sensitivity to batch and score scale}
Default and auxiliary runs come from different batches. We decompose within-configuration contrasts into a default-versus-auxiliary component and an auxiliary-versus-auxiliary component. The latter removes an offset shared by both auxiliary runs, while retaining one contrast per configuration. It doesn't confirm matching software provenance or remove differences shared with the selected checkpoint schedules, so a simulated batch offset provides a separate sensitivity check.

A positive multiplicative gain changes margin magnitudes while preserving their signs, and it leaves threshold accuracy unchanged by construction. We examine this mechanism with a gain covariate $s_{cr}$, the mean per-byte answer log-likelihood, and with simulations that vary the gain scale and margin distribution. We also fit a leave-one-benchmark-out competence proxy $x_{cr}^{(-j)}$ on the opposite item half, using
\begin{equation}
y_{crj}=\alpha_{cj}+\beta_jx_{cr}^{(-j)}+\gamma_js_{cr}+u_{crj}.
\end{equation}
The pooled fit uses one slope per benchmark over 250 run contrasts. These adjustments are descriptive, because the gain covariate shares item noise with the outcomes, and the competence proxy averages the same seed deviations it adjusts, so it removes shared covariance by construction. Appendix~\ref{app:proxy} shows that simulations with the observed seed covariance and no competence variable reproduce the repaired margin adjustments within 0.015, and we give none of them a mechanistic reading.

Per-benchmark reliability averages 0.652 on margins and 0.324 on accuracy. Seven accuracy benchmarks and three margin benchmarks have reliability below 0.50. WinoGrande has a negative estimated margin variance, and both PIQA and WinoGrande have negative accuracy estimates. The accuracy correlation estimate also contains an entry of 1.296 and an eigenvalue of $-0.55$, which makes it an invalid correlation matrix without further regularisation. The primary ratio uses covariance products directly, so it doesn't require this normalised matrix to be positive semidefinite.

Removing BoolQ raises margin inflation to 1.786 with interval \ci{1.689}{1.877}, and accuracy inflation to 1.558 with interval \ci{1.412}{1.687}. Removing CommonsenseQA instead gives inflation estimates of 1.096 and 1.014. BoolQ contributes 68\% of the margin covariance trace and 84\% of the accuracy trace. The removal estimates imply that the off-diagonal terms involving BoolQ are negative, and they sum to about $-0.15$ of the margin trace and $-0.06$ of the accuracy trace. Its removal therefore lowers both the numerator and the denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:lambda}, and the denominator falls by more. We recovered the 2,938 passages behind BoolQ's 3,270 questions from the release's request files and assigned each passage whole to one half. Across fifty re-splits this changes the two estimates by $-0.0002$ and $-0.0001$ on average, and the BoolQ diagonal moves by 0.03\% and 0.15\%, as Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} reports. Shared passages therefore don't account for its trace share, although that check can't reach other shared item effects. A permutation that reshuffles each run's item deviations inside every group leaves all trait scores fixed and breaks the tie between particular items and particular runs. Across 2,000 such permutations, 0.087 reach or exceed the observed margin estimate, so item-level sharing accounts for about 0.001 of it.

Recipe removal moves margin inflation between 1.207 and 1.256, and size removal moves it between 1.178 and 1.289, where deleting 530M gives \ci{0.993}{1.338} and stops excluding one. The corresponding accuracy ranges run from 1.065 to 1.099 and from 1.047 to 1.115. Benchmark-removal sensitivity is larger, and comparing that spread with a sampling standard error doesn't make it a distribution over benchmark collections, since our design treats this battery as fixed. Among equal-weight subsets of the same size, those containing BoolQ average between 0.115 and 0.596 lower margin inflation than those without it. Sweeping 2,000 weightings from a flat Dirichlet leaves 0.817 of margin intervals excluding one, and Appendix~\ref{app:composition} separates benchmark count, identity, and weights.

\label{sec:heldout}

The protocol named SciQ, MedMCQA, and two AGIEval reasoning tasks, with a rule that replaced any task whose 1B pilot accuracy fell below chance plus 0.02. Both AGIEval tasks missed that bar by about a thousandth, so the battery became SciQ, MedMCQA, and multiple-choice versions of DROP and CoQA. Each task has 1,000 items, and items that share a passage or story stay in the same half. Scoring all 375 released checkpoints would have exceeded our GPU budget, so the protocol's scope rule limited the test to the 225 runs at 530M, 750M, and 1B, at the steps of the primary analysis. Among the nine defined margin traits, mean estimated correlations are 0.624 within scoring formats and 0.007 across formats, and Figure~\ref{fig:covariance} shows the full correlation estimates with their eigenvalue spectra. Each benchmark appears in exactly one scoring format, and with that design we can't separate format from task content causally. Retaining only cross-format off-diagonal covariance gives a diagnostic inflation of 0.921 with interval \ci{0.801}{1.035}, compared with 1.244 for the full matrix. This diagnostic isn't the variance of a separately observed aggregate, because masking covariance entries needn't preserve positive semidefiniteness. The accuracy diagnostic is 0.965 with interval \ci{0.848}{1.063}, and removing BoolQ gives a cross-format margin diagnostic of 1.006, compared with 1.786 without the restriction.

We also transformed the saved margins item by item, holding items and runs fixed, to test whether the scoring rule alone can produce the gap between the two scales. Dividing each benchmark's margins by their pooled standard deviation gives margin inflation of 1.182 with interval \ci{1.094}{1.265}, bounding them with a hyperbolic tangent gives 1.141 with interval \ci{1.053}{1.222}, and keeping only their signs gives 1.079, as Appendix~\ref{app:scale} reports. These are interventions on the scoring rule and don't show how training produces large margins.

The auxiliary-only point estimates retain the full-battery inflation, although their intervals remain wide. The planned signature was margin inflation collapsing after gain adjustment. It didn't appear, although the proxy limitations restrict any interpretation of that failure. Adjusting for gain increases margin inflation to 1.275 and accuracy inflation to 1.097, joint adjustment gives 1.276 and 1.094, and competence adjustment alone gives 1.244 and 1.076.

At a gain standard deviation of 0.05, the default gain simulation produces margin inflation of 1.148, about 61\% of the observed excess above one. Because the default and distribution-matched simulators produce about 44\% and 6\% of that excess at the estimated gain standard deviation of 0.042, the share attributed to gain depends on the simulator chosen, and the recipe bootstrap interval for that standard deviation, 0.011 to 0.063, moves the default share from 2\% to 91\%. The simulations therefore provide no identified causal fraction for sharpness, and we obtain the simulated accuracy invariance from the definition of accuracy alone. Under the calibrated batch-offset simulation the factors are 1.004 and 1.003, about 1.5\% of the observed margin excess.

\subsection{Calibration and external checks}
Under independent noise the simulation gives mean inflation of 1.0009 on margins and 1.0004 on accuracy. The recovery slope across effective covariance coefficients from zero to 0.50 is 0.998, and fifty item re-splits have standard deviations of 0.002 and 0.008. These checks assess selected reference distributions and can't rule out training-schedule confounding.

The 500-permutation accuracy tail fraction is 0.028, and a later 1,000-permutation run gives 0.021. Both reach one in twenty, while the primary cluster interval contains one, so the two procedures disagree on accuracy. We retain both results because permutation exchangeability is stronger than a zero aggregate covariance condition, and because the permutation and cluster procedures condition on different aspects of the design.

PolyPythias gives margin inflation of 1.406 on the three planned sizes, compared with 1.244 for DataDecide. The ratio of excesses above one is 1.66, but the ratio of inflation factors is about 1.13. The reported transfer gate used the first scale after the plan left the comparison ambiguous, so it can't show failure of a 20\% aggregate-standard-deviation criterion. A five-size rescore, which we repeated and reproduced, gives margin inflation of 1.262 with interval \ci{0.927}{1.537} and accuracy inflation of 1.711 with interval \ci{0.869}{2.428}. Test inversion leaves accuracy unbounded, so these data don't bound PolyPythias accuracy inflation, as Appendix~\ref{app:transport} reports. Released single-configuration panels from \citet{heineman2025} give likelihood inflation of 1.705 and 1.625 and accuracy inflation of 0.989 and 0.868 under initialisation and data-order seeds. They lack cross-half correction and intervals, so they suggest the same score-scale difference without confirming the DataDecide estimand.

\subsection{Predicting aggregate uncertainty}
\label{sec:prediction}
We compare five covariance models and one exploratory rescaled plug-in on five folds of recipes left out of fitting, and Table~\ref{tab:prediction} in Appendix~\ref{app:paired} gives their errors. Each model receives the held-out marginal seed standard deviations, so the comparison isolates the correlation component. On margins, the rank-one, rank-one plus gain, and full models each lower the mean squared error of log aggregate standard deviation by about 0.03 from the 0.0358 of independence. Their paired recipe-bootstrap intervals exclude zero, although the rank-one interval reaches zero when nonpositive held-out variances are filled from training folds. On accuracy every interval for a difference from independence includes zero, and the unrescaled phenotypic plug-in has the largest error on both scales. An operational comparison that refits every matrix without held-out marginals and predicts each held-out configuration's raw variance moment finds no model that beats independence, as Appendix~\ref{app:paired} reports.

Under independent Gaussian run errors with a common aggregate standard deviation, a true score gap $\Delta$ reverses sign with probability
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{flip}}(\Delta)=\Phi\left(-\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{agg}}}\right).
\end{equation}
Using the reported accuracy deviation of 0.00514 gives probabilities of about 0.246 for a half-point gap and 0.085 for a one-point gap. Under independence, the same gaps give about 0.229 and 0.069. We computed these probabilities from the model without observing reversal rates, and unequal variances require a different denominator. Of 1,500 observed same-size gaps, the reported census places 455 below the corresponding 5\% risk threshold, though noisy gaps give no lower bound on the fraction of small true gaps. The same pairs have seed-matched runs, and the paired-difference standard deviation has a median ratio to its independence value of 1.024 on margins and 1.081 on accuracy. Matching seeds across configurations therefore doesn't reduce comparison noise in this population.

Simulated false-positive rates don't show how often the covariance changes a real decision, so we also checked recipe comparisons directly. At each size below 1B we called a pair of recipes when their aggregate gap exceeded two standard errors. We computed that standard error either under independence or with the estimated covariance, leaving the pair out of both estimates. We scored each call against the pair's ordering at 1B whenever that ordering itself cleared two corrected standard errors. Across the four sizes and both scales, the correction removes between 1 and 12 of the 169 to 223 calls on such pairs. It changes the share of wrong calls by at most 0.009, from rates between 0.029 and 0.070. Every recipe-bootstrap interval for that change reaches zero, as Appendix~\ref{app:decision} reports. A wrong call here also counts genuine rank changes between sizes, so these rates aren't false-positive rates, and they show that the correction seldom changes a decision in this population.

\section{Related work}
\citet{bouthillier2021} derive variance inflation from correlated repeated trainings of one pipeline on one task and measure the variance from each source in five case studies. We apply covariance accounting across benchmarks within a replicate run. \citet{reimers2017} and \citet{madaan2024} study seed-related evaluation variability, and \citet{dodge2020} examine whether favourable initialisations transfer across tasks. \citet{jordan2024} separates example-specific training variance from distribution-level variation and compares accuracies across related evaluation sets. Among the studies we located, none estimates the covariance of run noise between benchmarks or an effective benchmark count for a fixed battery. We measure that covariance with an established split-half construction applied to replicate runs.

The cross-half construction follows repeated-measurement and variance-component ideas from \citet{spearman1904}, \citet{cronbach1951}, and \citet{searle1992}, with the quantitative-genetics treatment of covariance structure \citep{martin1977} as an analogy that our model doesn't carry as far as separate variance components. \citet{cheverud1988} motivates the phenotypic plug-in comparison in Table~\ref{tab:prediction}, which fails here on the transfer of correlation structure.

\citet{heineman2025} measure per-benchmark signal and noise on DataDecide and OLMo checkpoints and relate the ratio to decision accuracy, without estimating covariance between benchmarks. \citet{magnusson2025} and \citet{vanderwal2025} provide the replicate populations used here. \citet{dehghani2021} show the consequences of benchmark composition, which our variance-weighted estimates also expose. Few-cluster inference follows the concerns studied by \citet{cameron2008} and \citet{mackinnon2017}. Appendix~\ref{app:design} discusses further related approaches and their different units of replication.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
Accuracy inflation is small for the full DataDecide battery, but removing BoolQ raises the estimate, and a true ratio of 1.10 would have been declared in only 0.135 of replicates, so that interval bounds a large ratio rather than showing independence. On margins, the estimate depends on scoring format and gain simulation assumptions, and after these sensitivities we lack sufficient evidence for a general approximation of independent benchmark noise.

Apart from the registered test, which reached one of its three sizes, the empirical study remains exploratory, since we used the full dataset without the planned screening split. The passage-aware split bounds BoolQ passage leakage within re-split variation, and common-step scoring still leaves differences between training schedules. A variance floor let WinoGrande dominate the shipped margin proxy, and repaired margin proxies built from the same seed deviations fall below one even in simulations without a separate competence variable. We make no claim about the competence mechanism, which needs held-out loss scored on the same checkpoints.

We cluster on recipes, and because the release shares one seed label across every recipe inside a size band, a run effect that follows the size band would break that choice, taking simulated coverage of the recipe-clustered interval to 0.821 at a quarter of latent variance and 0.580 at a half. A permutation test aligning runs by batch slot bounds that effect at 0.044 of the seed covariance on margins, where simulated coverage is 0.943. Clustering on the five size bands instead leaves both conclusions standing, at \ci{1.030}{1.426} for margins and \ci{0.961}{1.184} for accuracy, though the reported widths don't survive. Within a single size the evidence is weaker, because the 1B margin estimate of 1.149 has a cluster-robust log-scale interval of 0.811 to 1.628 that includes one, and a wider wild-bootstrap one, although all five bands exclude one on margins once BoolQ is removed, as Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary} reports.

For practice we recommend paired replicate scores across the chosen battery, an aggregate seed standard deviation, and a benchmark-removal sensitivity check. An independence rule declares a margin difference in 0.113 of simulated comparisons with a true gap of zero. Scaling its standard error by a ratio estimated on the same population returns that to 0.051 at any run budget, although a covariance-based standard error changed few of the real recipe calls above. Carried to a battery whose own ratio is 1.5, that ratio leaves the error at 0.101, so it has to be measured locally. In simulations at the observed mean seed correlation, the margin interval excludes one in 0.86 of populations with two runs per configuration and in 0.997 with three, while accuracy needs eight runs to reach 0.803.
