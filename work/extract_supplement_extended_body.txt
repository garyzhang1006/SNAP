Reimers and Gurevych (2017) compare score distributions across seeds within datasets, while Madaan et al (2024) report benchmark-level seed variance. Hagmann et al (2023) model nondeterminism with scalar variance components and their interactions with data properties. Our target adds covariance between benchmark-specific run deviations to that measurement problem.

Jordan (2024) attributes much test-set variance to per-example variation across seeds, and its Appendix C compares run accuracy across five ImageNet evaluation sets with mathematical-expression at most 0p14. Those comparisons concern shifted versions of one benchmark. Bui et al (2025) vary fine-tuning seeds, and Hochlehnert et al (2025) vary decoding seeds, which address different variance components from the pretraining-run variation examined here. Dodge et al (2020) ask whether a seed performs well across datasets and compare four GLUE task rankings, but they don't estimate a covariance matrix.

Zhou et al (2020) report a Spearman correlation matrix across datasets. Their standard block has correlations at least 0p95, while standard-to-analysis entries include 0p97 for STR-L and 0p30 for STR-O. These correlations compare checkpoints within runs and average across seeds. They mix changes in checkpoint performance with within-configuration seed deviations and can't isolate the latter. Dufour et al (2026) separate training and sampling variation in FID, while Miller (2024) covers item-sampling uncertainty with clustered and paired two-model comparisons and treats each evaluated model as fixed. Zhao et al (2026) report the three-seed standard deviation of a twelve-task average and combine twelve per-task Welch tests in their Appendix E.4, but they don't estimate cross-task covariance.

Haupt et al (2026) use the PolyPythias 410M panel to estimate per-item variation and prioritise items by welfare weight and improvability. Their per-item variance estimates and approximate item-independence assumption address a different quantity from the off-diagonal covariance of benchmark run effects. Their item-level estimates and our benchmark covariance therefore answer different questions using some of the same models.

Cheverud (2001), Nyholt (2004), and Li and Ji (2005) estimate effective numbers of independent tests from observed correlation matrices. Cheverud uses eigenvalue variance, Nyholt uses spectral decomposition of linkage disequilibrium, and Li and Ji revise the effective-count approximation. Their application adjusts significance thresholds, whereas ours converts an estimated noise covariance into an aggregate standard deviation and an effective benchmark count. Searle et al (1992) explain negative variance-component estimates from moment methods when the true component lies near zero.

Schaeffer et al (2023) show how discontinuous metrics can change the appearance of performance trends. Accuracy thresholds our margin at zero, so we report both scales and retain their separate noise properties. Low estimated seed signal relative to item noise provides a measurement limitation for accuracy in this study. Dehghani et al (2021) show that benchmark selection can reorder methods, which concerns the composition sensitivity examined here.

Demsar (2006) and Dror et al (2017) develop inference across datasets, while Card et al (2020) examine statistical power and Gorman and Bedrick (2019) examine dependence on a single split. Burnell et al (2023) advocate per-instance reports alongside aggregate scores, and Henderson et al (2018) and Agarwal et al (2021) address seed sensitivity and uncertainty in reinforcement learning. Both resample runs within tasks, while our application requires preservation of paired run effects across benchmarks.

Hardy et al (2026) apply generalizability theory to 4,000 released models, which places replication across models and never across training seeds. Kohli (2026) estimates roughly two independent votes among nine frontier judges, and Messing (2026) reports naive standard errors that are 40 to 60 percent too small once the analysis accounts for judge and prompt variation. Sha and Zhao (2026) estimate how many independent signals a leaderboard's benchmark scores provide across models, which counts variation between different models, while our effective count describes the run-to-run covariance at a fixed configuration. Those studies measure dependence across models, judges, or prompts, while we measure paired benchmark deviations across training runs.

The cross-half identity builds on the repeated-measurement approach to attenuation described by Spearman (1904). Under independent errors between halves, paired measurements remove the item-noise contribution from covariance estimates, diagonal entries included. Cronbach (1951) relates split-half reliability to random halves of an item population, which motivates the reliability table and the 50 re-splits in N3. Those re-splits change the margin estimate by a reported standard deviation of 0p002.

The Brown (1910) and Spearman (1910) test-length formula contains mathematical-expression , the same algebraic form used for mathematical-expression . Its correlation concerns parallel items, whereas ours concerns benchmark run effects. A substitution of the reliability means in Table 1 at mathematical-expression would produce 6p87 and 3p91, which aren't estimates of our variance inflation.

Campbell and Fiske (1959) compare traits and measurement methods in a crossed matrix. The within-format and cross-format summaries here resemble heterotrait blocks, but no benchmark appears in multiple formats. This design therefore can't separate benchmark content from scoring format or establish format as the cause of the association. Brennan (2001) provides the variance-component framework for these distinctions.

Hofmann et al (2025) fit benchmark-specific item-response models to an overlapping evaluation panel, with seven authors shared with Heineman et al (2025). Kipnis et al (2024) estimate a factor across six benchmarks that correlates 0p94 with total score. Those ability structures describe performance differences, a separate quantity from the within-configuration noise covariance we estimate here. Hedges et al (2010) estimate uncertainty for dependent effect sizes and don't require their complete covariance matrix, while our application estimates the covariance needed for an effective-count point estimate.
